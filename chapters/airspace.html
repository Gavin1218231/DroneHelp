Chapter 2

Airspace & Sectional Charts

Understanding airspace classifications, reading sectional charts, and knowing when ATC authorization is required.

Visual Overview

Airspace & Sectional Charts Overview

The National Airspace System (NAS) is divided into different classes of airspace, each with specific rules governing who can fly where. As a Part 107 remote pilot, understanding these classifications is essential for legal and safe drone operations.

In this chapter you will learn:

  • The difference between controlled and uncontrolled airspace
  • The six airspace classes (A, B, C, D, E, and G) and their relevance to Part 107
  • How to identify airspace on VFR Sectional Charts
  • Special use airspace and temporary flight restrictions
  • How to obtain authorization to fly in controlled airspace using LAANC
Key Point: Airspace questions make up approximately 15–25% of the Part 107 exam. Knowing how to read a sectional chart is one of the most testable skills.

Two Categories: Controlled vs. Uncontrolled

All airspace in the United States falls into one of two broad categories:

Controlled Airspace (Classes A, B, C, D, and E)

  • Air Traffic Control (ATC) provides separation services for some or all aircraft
  • Part 107 pilots need ATC authorization before operating in Classes B, C, D, and surface-level E airspace
  • Specific weather minimums and communication requirements apply

Uncontrolled Airspace (Class G)

  • ATC does not exercise authority over air traffic
  • No ATC authorization required for Part 107 operations
  • Where the majority of Part 107 drone operations take place
Memory Aid: Think of it as a letter grade system — A through E are controlled (someone is "grading" you), and G stands for "Go ahead" (uncontrolled).

Class A Airspace

Class A airspace covers the entire United States from 18,000 feet MSL up to and including Flight Level 600 (approximately 60,000 feet MSL).

  • All operations must be conducted under Instrument Flight Rules (IFR)
  • ATC clearance is required at all times
  • All aircraft must be equipped with a transponder and altitude-reporting equipment
  • Pilots must hold an instrument rating
Part 107 Relevance: Class A airspace is NOT relevant to Part 107 operations. Part 107 restricts drone flights to a maximum altitude of 400 feet AGL (well below 18,000 feet MSL). Class A airspace is not depicted on VFR Sectional Charts because VFR flight is not permitted within it.

Class B Airspace

Class B airspace surrounds the nation's busiest airports (such as LAX, JFK, ORD, ATL).

  • Chart depiction: Solid blue lines
  • Shape: Often described as an "upside-down wedding cake" with multiple shelves extending outward at progressively higher altitudes
  • Typical dimensions: Surface to 10,000 feet MSL, radius up to 30 nautical miles
  • All aircraft require explicit ATC clearance to enter
  • Mode C transponder required within and above the Class B area (Mode C veil extends 30 NM)
Part 107 Rule: ATC authorization is REQUIRED before operating a drone in Class B airspace. Authorization can be obtained through LAANC or the FAA DroneZone.
Chart example: On a sectional chart you will see concentric solid blue circles around major airports with altitude labels like "100/SFC" meaning the floor is the surface and the ceiling is 10,000 feet MSL.

Class C Airspace

Class C airspace surrounds airports with an operational control tower, radar approach control, and a certain level of IFR or passenger operations.

  • Chart depiction: Solid magenta lines
  • Shape: Two-shelf structure (inner and outer shelves)
  • Inner shelf: Surface to typically 4,000 feet AGL, 5 NM radius
  • Outer shelf: Typically 1,200 feet AGL to 4,000 feet AGL, 10 NM radius
  • Two-way radio communication with ATC is required
  • An outer area (20 NM) exists where participation is encouraged but not required
Part 107 Rule: ATC authorization is REQUIRED before operating a drone in Class C airspace. Use LAANC for near real-time approval.

Class D Airspace

Class D airspace surrounds airports that have an operational control tower but may not have radar approach control.

  • Chart depiction: Dashed blue lines
  • Shape: Cylindrical, typically 4 NM radius from the airport
  • Ceiling: Shown as a blue number inside a dashed blue box (e.g., "[25]" means 2,500 feet MSL)
  • Two-way radio communication must be established with the tower before entry
  • When the control tower is not in operation, the airspace typically reverts to Class E or Class G
Part 107 Rule: ATC authorization is REQUIRED before operating a drone in Class D airspace. Always check tower operating hours — when the tower closes, the airspace class changes.
Exam Tip: The Part 107 exam may ask what happens to Class D airspace when the tower closes. The answer is it reverts to Class E surface area (if a dashed magenta line is shown) or Class G airspace.

Class E Airspace

Class E is the most common type of controlled airspace in the United States. It exists wherever controlled airspace is needed but is not classified as A, B, C, or D.

Chart Depictions for Different Class E Floors:

  • Surface (SFC): Dashed magenta lines — Class E starts at the surface. ATC authorization is required for Part 107 here.
  • 700 feet AGL floor: Faded/shaded magenta (vignette) on the chart side where Class E begins at 700 feet AGL
  • 1,200 feet AGL floor: Blue shading (vignette) — Class E begins at 1,200 feet AGL. This is the default floor in most of the US.
  • 14,500 feet MSL: In remote/mountainous areas, Class E may not begin until 14,500 feet MSL
Part 107 Rule: Authorization is ONLY required in Class E airspace that extends to the surface (dashed magenta lines). If Class E begins at 700 or 1,200 feet AGL, Part 107 operations below 400 feet AGL do not require authorization (but you are still in Class G at that altitude).

Class G Airspace

Class G is uncontrolled airspace. ATC has no authority or responsibility for controlling air traffic within it.

  • Chart depiction: No specific marking — it exists wherever other controlled airspace has not been designated
  • Extends from the surface up to the base of overlying controlled airspace (usually the floor of Class E)
  • Most common at lower altitudes, particularly in rural and sparsely populated areas
  • No ATC authorization is needed for Part 107 operations
Part 107 Rule: The majority of Part 107 drone operations take place in Class G airspace. No ATC authorization is needed, but you must still follow all other Part 107 rules (400 ft AGL max, visual line of sight, etc.).
How to find Class G: On a sectional chart, if you see faded magenta shading, the airspace below 700 feet AGL is Class G. If you see blue shading, the airspace below 1,200 feet AGL is Class G. No shading at all means Class G extends up to 14,500 feet MSL.

Special Use Airspace (SUA)

Special Use Airspace areas impose additional limitations on aircraft operations. These are critical for Part 107 pilots to understand.

  • Prohibited Areas (P): Flight is completely prohibited (e.g., P-56 over the White House). Depicted with blue hatching on charts.
  • Restricted Areas (R): Contain hazards such as artillery firing, aerial gunnery, or guided missiles. Entry requires permission from the controlling agency. Blue hatching with "R" designation.
  • Military Operations Areas (MOAs): Military training activities. VFR traffic is not prohibited but should exercise extreme caution. Depicted with magenta hatching.
  • Warning Areas (W): Similar to Restricted Areas but located over international waters (beyond 3 NM from the coast).
  • Alert Areas (A): Areas of high pilot training activity or unusual aerial activity. No restrictions, but heightened awareness required.
  • Controlled Firing Areas (CFAs): Not depicted on charts. Activities are suspended when spotters detect approaching aircraft.
Part 107 Rule: NEVER fly in Prohibited Areas. For Restricted Areas, contact the controlling agency before flying. Always check NOTAMs for active MOA and Warning Area schedules.

TFRs & NOTAMs

Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs) and Notices to Air Missions (NOTAMs) are critical pre-flight checks for every Part 107 operation.

Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)

  • Imposed for presidential movements (VIP TFRs), sporting events, wildfires, disaster/hazard areas, space launches, and national security
  • VIP TFRs typically include a 30 NM outer ring and a 10 NM inner ring of restricted airspace
  • Stadium TFRs extend 3 NM radius and up to 3,000 feet AGL around major sporting events with 30,000+ capacity
  • Can be issued with short notice — always check before every flight

NOTAMs (Notices to Air Missions)

  • Provide time-critical aeronautical information about temporary changes
  • Include runway closures, navaid outages, airspace changes, and TFR details
  • Part 107 pilots must check NOTAMs as part of their pre-flight planning
Always check TFRs before flying! Sources include the FAA TFR website (tfr.faa.gov), the FAA B4UFLY app, and LAANC-approved apps such as Aloft (formerly Kittyhawk) or AirMap.

LAANC Authorization

LAANC stands for Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability. It is the FAA's system for providing near real-time airspace authorization to Part 107 pilots.

How LAANC Works:

  • Pilots submit an authorization request through an FAA-approved LAANC service supplier (e.g., Aloft, AirMap, DJI Fly, Skyward)
  • The system evaluates the request against pre-approved UAS Facility Maps (UASFM)
  • If the requested altitude is at or below the pre-approved ceiling for that grid cell, authorization is granted automatically (usually within seconds)
  • If above the pre-approved ceiling, a manual "Further Coordination" request is sent to the FAA (may take up to 90 days)

Key LAANC Facts:

  • Available at nearly 740 FAA air traffic facilities covering approximately 500 airports
  • Authorizations are valid for a specific location, altitude, date, and time window
  • LAANC does NOT replace the requirement to register your drone or hold a Part 107 certificate
Alternative to LAANC: Pilots can also request airspace authorization manually through the FAA DroneZone portal at faadronezone.faa.gov. Manual approvals typically take much longer (up to 90 days).

Reading Sectional Charts — Key Symbols

VFR Sectional Charts are the primary tool for identifying airspace boundaries, obstacles, and other aeronautical information relevant to drone operations.

Airport Symbols

  • Solid circle (magenta): Airport without a control tower (uncontrolled)
  • Solid circle (blue): Airport with an operating control tower (towered)
  • Open circle: Hard-surfaced runway; solid fill indicates other surfaces

Obstacle Markings

  • Obstacles (towers, antennas, buildings) are shown with a vertical line and dot symbol
  • Two numbers appear: the top number (bold) is the height MSL, and the bottom number (in parentheses) is the height AGL
  • Group obstacles are shown with a special symbol indicating the tallest in the group

Altitude References

  • MSL (Mean Sea Level): Measured from sea level; used for airspace floors/ceilings
  • AGL (Above Ground Level): Measured from the ground surface; Part 107 altitude limit (400 ft) is AGL
Exam Tip: The Part 107 exam frequently tests whether you can distinguish between MSL and AGL altitudes on a sectional chart. Always pay attention to which reference is being used.

In-Depth Reading

Introduction to the National Airspace System

The National Airspace System (NAS) encompasses every component used to manage air traffic safely and efficiently across the United States. For Part 107 remote pilots, understanding how the NAS is organized is not just an exam requirement — it is a fundamental safety skill. Operating a drone in the wrong airspace without authorization can result in enforcement actions, certificate revocation, and poses a serious threat to manned aircraft.

The NAS is divided into two primary categories of airspace: controlled and uncontrolled. Within controlled airspace, there are five classes designated by the letters A through E. Uncontrolled airspace is designated as Class G. Each class has specific operating rules, weather minimums, pilot requirements, and equipment mandates. Additionally, there are several types of special use airspace and other airspace areas that impose unique restrictions.

The primary tool for identifying airspace on the ground is the VFR Sectional Aeronautical Chart, published by the FAA every 56 days. These charts use standardized symbology and color-coding to depict airspace boundaries, airports, obstacles, navigation aids, and terrain. Part 107 pilots must be able to read and interpret these charts to determine what airspace they are operating in and whether authorization is needed.

Airspace Classification Summary

The following table provides a quick-reference summary of all six airspace classes and their key attributes relevant to Part 107 operations:

Class Type Chart Indicator Altitude Range Part 107 Auth Required?
A Controlled Not depicted on VFR charts 18,000 ft MSL – FL600 N/A (drones cannot operate here)
B Controlled Solid blue lines Surface – typically 10,000 ft MSL Yes
C Controlled Solid magenta lines Surface – typically 4,000 ft AGL Yes
D Controlled Dashed blue lines Surface – ceiling in blue box (MSL) Yes
E Controlled Dashed magenta (sfc), magenta shading (700 ft), blue shading (1,200 ft) Varies (surface, 700 ft AGL, or 1,200 ft AGL up to but not including 18,000 ft MSL) Only if extending to surface
G Uncontrolled No specific marking Surface up to overlying Class E floor No

Class A Airspace in Detail

Class A airspace extends from 18,000 feet MSL (often expressed as Flight Level 180) up to and including Flight Level 600 (approximately 60,000 feet MSL). This airspace blankets the entire contiguous United States, Alaska, and the surrounding oceanic areas within 12 nautical miles of the coastline.

All operations within Class A must be conducted under Instrument Flight Rules (IFR), and pilots must receive an ATC clearance before entering. Because Part 107 limits drone operations to a maximum of 400 feet AGL (and only higher if within 400 feet of a structure), Class A airspace has no practical relevance to sUAS operations. It is not depicted on VFR Sectional Charts because VFR flight is not permitted within it.

Note: While Class A is not directly relevant to Part 107 operations, the exam may include it as a distractor answer. Know that it exists above 18,000 feet MSL and requires IFR operations.

Class B Airspace in Detail

Class B airspace is established around the nation's busiest airports in terms of IFR operations and passenger traffic. Examples include airports like Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (ATL), Los Angeles International (LAX), Chicago O'Hare (ORD), and John F. Kennedy International (JFK). There are approximately 37 Class B airports in the United States.

On a VFR Sectional Chart, Class B airspace is depicted with solid blue lines. The shape is often described as an "upside-down wedding cake" because the airspace extends outward in multiple tiers as altitude increases. Each tier is labeled with a fraction showing the ceiling over the floor in hundreds of feet MSL. For example, "100/30" means the ceiling of that tier is 10,000 feet MSL and the floor is 3,000 feet MSL.

The innermost tier typically extends from the surface up to the overall ceiling (e.g., surface to 10,000 feet MSL). As you move outward from the airport, the floor rises and the airspace extends over a larger area. The entire structure can extend up to 30 nautical miles from the airport center.

For manned aircraft, an explicit ATC clearance with the phrase "cleared into the Class Bravo" is required before entry. A Mode C transponder is required within Class B airspace and within the Mode C veil (a 30 NM radius ring around the primary airport).

Part 107 Requirement: Remote pilots must obtain ATC authorization before operating a drone in Class B airspace. This is most easily accomplished through LAANC. Be aware that many grid cells within Class B have a UASFM ceiling of 0, meaning no automatic LAANC approval is available and you must submit a Further Coordination request through the FAA DroneZone.

Class C Airspace in Detail

Class C airspace surrounds airports that have an operational control tower, radar approach control services, and meet specific criteria for IFR operations or passenger enplanements. There are approximately 120 Class C airports in the United States.

On a sectional chart, Class C is depicted with solid magenta lines. The typical structure consists of two concentric shelves:

  • Inner shelf (surface area): 5 NM radius from the airport, surface to 4,000 feet AGL (though this varies)
  • Outer shelf: 10 NM radius from the airport, 1,200 feet AGL to 4,000 feet AGL

Beyond these two shelves, there is often a non-charted outer area extending 20 NM from the airport where radar services are available on a workload-permitting basis. While this outer area is not technically part of Class C airspace, pilots are encouraged to participate.

Like Class B, each ring is labeled with a fraction in hundreds of feet MSL (e.g., "41/SFC" means ceiling is 4,100 feet MSL, floor is the surface). Two-way radio communication must be established with ATC before entry — simply hearing ATC acknowledge your call sign is sufficient (unlike Class B, which requires a specific clearance).

Tip: To distinguish Class B from Class C on a chart, remember: B = Blue solid lines, C = magenta (think "Crimson/Cherry") solid lines. Both use solid lines, but the color is different.

Class D Airspace in Detail

Class D airspace surrounds airports that have an operational control tower but do not necessarily have radar approach control. These are typically medium-sized airports with moderate traffic levels. There are several hundred Class D airports across the United States.

On a sectional chart, Class D is depicted with dashed blue lines forming a roughly circular boundary around the airport, typically with a 4 NM radius. The ceiling of Class D airspace is indicated by a number enclosed in a dashed blue box near the airport symbol. This number represents the ceiling in hundreds of feet MSL. For example, a number "[25]" means the ceiling is 2,500 feet MSL.

Two-way radio communication must be established with the control tower before entering Class D airspace. The key difference from Class B is that only communication (not clearance) is needed, and unlike Class C, the airspace structure is simpler with no multi-shelf arrangement.

An important characteristic of Class D is that it only exists when the control tower is operational. When the tower closes (for example, during nighttime hours at airports without 24-hour towers), the airspace reverts to another classification:

  • If a dashed magenta line is shown around or near the airport, the airspace becomes Class E surface area
  • If no dashed magenta line is present, the airspace typically reverts to Class G
Exam Alert: Questions about what happens when a Class D tower closes are very common on the Part 107 exam. Remember that dashed blue (Class D) plus dashed magenta (Class E surface) means the area remains controlled even after the tower closes.

Class E Airspace in Detail

Class E is the most expansive type of controlled airspace in the United States. It exists wherever controlled airspace is needed for IFR transition, airways, and approach procedures but does not meet the criteria for a higher classification. Understanding the various "floors" of Class E is one of the most challenging topics for Part 107 candidates.

Class E can begin at several different altitudes, and the chart depiction varies accordingly:

Class E Starting at the Surface

Depicted by a dashed magenta line around an area (often an airport). This designation is commonly used for airports that need controlled airspace for instrument approaches but do not have an operating control tower (or after a Class D tower closes). Part 107 pilots must obtain ATC authorization to operate within Class E surface areas.

Class E Starting at 700 feet AGL

Depicted by a faded (vignette) magenta shading on the chart. The magenta shading appears on the side where Class E begins at 700 feet AGL. On the other side of the line, Class E typically starts at a higher altitude or at the surface. Below 700 feet AGL in these areas, the airspace is Class G. Since Part 107 operations are limited to 400 feet AGL, pilots typically operate within the Class G portion and do not need ATC authorization.

Class E Starting at 1,200 feet AGL

This is the default floor for Class E airspace across most of the United States. On sectional charts, you can identify areas where the floor transitions from 700 feet to 1,200 feet by looking for blue vignette (faded blue) shading. The blue shading appears on the side where Class E starts at 1,200 feet AGL. Below 1,200 feet AGL, the airspace is Class G. No Part 107 authorization is needed.

Class E Starting at 14,500 feet MSL

In remote and mountainous areas (primarily in the western United States), Class E may not begin until 14,500 feet MSL. This means Class G extends from the surface all the way up to 14,500 feet MSL. These areas are not specifically shaded on sectional charts — the absence of any magenta or blue vignette indicates the higher Class E floor.

Key Distinction for Part 107: The only Class E scenario requiring ATC authorization is when Class E extends to the surface (dashed magenta line). In all other cases, Part 107 drones fly within the Class G layer below the Class E floor and no authorization is needed.

Class G Airspace in Detail

Class G is the only uncontrolled airspace in the NAS. ATC does not have authority or responsibility for separating aircraft operating within Class G. It has no specific depiction on sectional charts — rather, it is defined as the airspace that exists from the surface up to the base of overlying controlled airspace (usually the floor of Class E).

The vertical extent of Class G depends on the Class E floor above it:

  • Where Class E begins at the surface (dashed magenta line): No Class G exists at that location
  • Where Class E begins at 700 feet AGL (magenta vignette): Class G extends from the surface to 700 feet AGL
  • Where Class E begins at 1,200 feet AGL (blue vignette or no shading): Class G extends from the surface to 1,200 feet AGL
  • Where Class E begins at 14,500 feet MSL (remote areas): Class G extends from the surface up to 14,500 feet MSL

The vast majority of Part 107 drone operations take place in Class G airspace, particularly in suburban and rural areas. While no ATC authorization is needed, all other Part 107 regulations still apply, including the 400-foot AGL altitude limit, visual line-of-sight requirements, and yield-right-of-way rules for manned aircraft.

Tip: Although Class G is uncontrolled, that does not mean "unregulated." Part 107 rules, federal laws, and state/local ordinances still apply. Additionally, other pilots (manned aircraft operating VFR) may be flying in Class G at low altitudes, so vigilance and a visual observer can enhance safety.

How to Read Sectional Charts

VFR Sectional Charts are large-format aeronautical charts produced by the FAA and published every 56 days. They use a Lambert Conformal Conic projection at a scale of 1:500,000. For Part 107 pilots, the ability to interpret these charts is one of the most heavily tested skills on the exam.

Airport Symbols

Airports are depicted on sectional charts using standardized symbols that convey key information at a glance:

  • Blue airport symbol: Airport with an operating control tower (towered/controlled)
  • Magenta airport symbol: Airport without an operating control tower (non-towered/uncontrolled)
  • Open circle: Hard-surfaced runways (paved)
  • Solid/filled circle: Other than hard-surfaced runways (turf, gravel, etc.)
  • Runway pattern shown: Airports with runways longer than 8,069 feet have the actual runway layout depicted to scale

Adjacent to each airport symbol, you will find data blocks that include the airport name, its identifier (e.g., KORD), the field elevation in feet MSL, the CTAF/UNICOM frequency, runway length (longest runway in hundreds of feet), and lighting information. Understanding this data is important for situational awareness, especially when operating near airports.

Obstacle Markings

Man-made obstacles such as communication towers, smokestacks, wind turbines, and tall buildings are depicted on sectional charts when they meet certain height criteria. The standard symbology includes:

  • A vertical line with a dot at the top (representing the obstacle)
  • Two altitude numbers: The bold number on top is the height in feet MSL, and the number in parentheses below is the height in feet AGL
  • Lighted obstacle: A lightning bolt or star symbol indicates the obstacle has lighting
  • Group of obstacles: A special symbol with the text "GROUP OBST" identifies clusters, with the tallest obstacle height shown
  • High-intensity obstacles: Obstacles 1,000 feet AGL or higher are shown with a distinct bold symbol
Exam Tip: Be careful not to confuse MSL and AGL when reading obstacle heights on a sectional chart. The exam will often provide a chart excerpt and ask for the obstacle height AGL — that is the number in parentheses. The MSL value is the bold number on top.

Airspace Boundary Lines and Colors

The following summary connects chart markings to airspace classes for quick identification:

Chart Feature Color/Style Airspace Indication
Solid lines around airport Blue Class B boundary
Solid lines around airport Magenta Class C boundary
Dashed lines around airport Blue Class D boundary
Dashed lines around area Magenta Class E surface area
Faded/vignette shading Magenta Class E floor at 700 ft AGL
Faded/vignette shading Blue Class E floor at 1,200 ft AGL
Number in dashed box Blue Class D ceiling (hundreds of feet MSL)
Fraction label (e.g., 100/40) Blue Class B shelf ceiling/floor (hundreds of feet MSL)

Frequencies and Communication

Sectional charts display various frequencies near airports and along airways. While Part 107 pilots do not typically communicate via radio, understanding these frequencies is helpful:

  • CT (Control Tower): The primary tower frequency for towered airports
  • CTAF (Common Traffic Advisory Frequency): Shown with a circled "C" after the frequency number. Used at non-towered airports for traffic advisories
  • ATIS (Automatic Terminal Information Service): Provides current weather and airport information on a recorded broadcast
  • UNICOM: Advisory frequency at airports without a tower

LAANC and FAA DroneZone

The Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability (LAANC) was developed by the FAA to streamline the process of granting airspace authorizations to Part 107 remote pilots. Before LAANC, pilots had to submit requests through the FAA DroneZone portal and wait weeks or even months for approval. LAANC enables near real-time authorization in many cases.

How LAANC Works

The system uses UAS Facility Maps (UASFMs), which are FAA-published maps that divide the airspace around airports into a grid of cells. Each cell has a pre-approved maximum altitude for drone operations. When a Part 107 pilot submits a LAANC request through an approved service supplier, the system checks whether the requested flight altitude falls within the pre-approved ceiling for that grid cell:

  • At or below the UASFM ceiling: Authorization is granted automatically, typically within seconds
  • Above the UASFM ceiling (or ceiling is 0): The request enters "Further Coordination" and is manually reviewed by FAA personnel, which can take up to 90 days

LAANC Service Suppliers

The FAA has approved several LAANC service suppliers. These include apps and platforms such as Aloft (formerly Kittyhawk), AirMap, DJI Fly, Skyward (Verizon), DroneUp, and others. Each provides an interface for submitting authorization requests, viewing UAS Facility Maps, and receiving approval notifications.

FAA DroneZone

The FAA DroneZone (faadronezone.faa.gov) is the FAA's official online portal for drone-related services. Through DroneZone, pilots can:

  • Register their drones (required for all drones weighing between 0.55 lbs and 55 lbs)
  • Submit airspace authorization requests (for locations not covered by LAANC or for altitudes above UASFM ceilings)
  • Apply for Part 107 waivers (such as night operations, operations over people, or beyond visual line of sight)
  • Report drone accidents meeting the reporting threshold
Practical Tip: Always use LAANC when available, as it is much faster than the DroneZone manual process. Download a LAANC app before your flight to check airspace restrictions and request authorization on-site. Even if you are flying in Class G, these apps are valuable for checking TFRs and other restrictions.

Special Use Airspace in Detail

Special Use Airspace (SUA) areas exist to confine certain activities that may be hazardous to other aircraft. Part 107 pilots must be aware of these areas and the restrictions they impose:

Prohibited Areas

Designated with a "P" prefix (e.g., P-56A and P-56B over the White House and Capitol complex, P-49 over Camp David). Flight of any aircraft, including drones, is strictly prohibited. These areas are depicted on sectional charts with blue hatching and a label such as "P-56A." There is no process for obtaining authorization to fly in a Prohibited Area — it is a hard restriction.

Restricted Areas

Designated with an "R" prefix (e.g., R-2301 at White Sands Missile Range). These areas contain activities such as artillery firing, aerial gunnery, missile testing, or other hazardous operations. Entry requires permission from the controlling agency. On sectional charts, they appear with blue hatching and are labeled with their designation, altitudes, and controlling agency. Time of use is published in a table on the chart margin or in NOTAMs.

Military Operations Areas (MOAs)

MOAs are established to separate military training activities from IFR traffic. VFR traffic (including drones in Class G below the MOA floor) is not prohibited, but pilots should exercise extreme caution due to high-speed military aircraft operating in the area. MOAs are depicted with magenta hatching on sectional charts and are labeled with their name and altitudes. Activity schedules are listed in the chart margin or available through FSS (Flight Service Station).

Warning Areas

Warning Areas are similar to Restricted Areas in terms of hazardous activities, but they are located over international waters (beyond 3 NM from the coast). They are designated with a "W" prefix. While the FAA cannot legally restrict flight in international airspace, pilots are strongly cautioned. Part 107 operations in warning areas would be unusual since drones must remain within visual line of sight.

Alert Areas

Alert Areas are established where there is a high volume of pilot training activity or unusual aerial operations (such as parachute jumping). There are no restrictions on flying through an Alert Area, but heightened awareness is essential. They are designated with an "A" prefix and depicted on charts with identifying information.

Controlled Firing Areas (CFAs)

CFAs contain activities such as ground-based weapons firing that could pose a hazard to aircraft. The key distinction is that CFAs are not depicted on sectional charts. Activities within CFAs are suspended whenever a spotter, radar, or other observation method detects an approaching aircraft, making them inherently self-correcting.

Summary of SUA Chart Depictions: Prohibited and Restricted areas use blue hatching. MOAs use magenta hatching. Warning and Alert areas also have identifying boundaries. CFAs are not shown on charts. Always consult the chart legend and NOTAMs for active times and controlling agencies.

Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)

Temporary Flight Restrictions are among the most important pre-flight considerations for Part 107 pilots. TFRs are established for a limited time to restrict air traffic in a defined area for safety, security, or other public interest reasons. Unlike permanent airspace designations, TFRs can appear with little notice and change rapidly.

Common Types of TFRs

  • VIP TFRs (14 CFR 91.141): Issued for the President, Vice President, and other high-ranking officials. Typically include a 10 NM inner ring (no flight operations) and a 30 NM outer ring (restrictions apply). These are among the most strictly enforced TFRs.
  • Stadium/Sporting Event TFRs (14 CFR 91.145): Prohibit flight within a 3 NM radius and up to 3,000 feet AGL of major sporting events at stadiums with a seating capacity of 30,000 or more, from one hour before to one hour after the event.
  • Disaster/Hazard TFRs (14 CFR 91.137): Established over natural disasters (wildfires, hurricanes, earthquakes), accident sites, or other hazard areas to provide a safe operating environment for emergency response aircraft.
  • Space Operations TFRs (14 CFR 91.143): Issued for rocket launches and recoveries at sites like Kennedy Space Center and Vandenberg Space Force Base.
  • National Security TFRs (14 CFR 99.7): Established for reasons of national defense, such as around Washington, D.C. (the DC SFRA and FRZ).

How to Check for TFRs

Part 107 pilots must check for TFRs before every flight. Reliable sources include:

  • FAA TFR website: tfr.faa.gov provides a current list and graphical depiction of all active and upcoming TFRs
  • FAA B4UFLY app: A free app that provides airspace and TFR information based on your location
  • LAANC apps: Aloft, AirMap, and other LAANC providers integrate TFR data into their maps
  • Flight Service (1-800-WX-BRIEF): Pilots can call for a briefing that includes active NOTAMs and TFRs
Critical Warning: Flying in a TFR without authorization is a serious violation that can result in certificate suspension or revocation, civil penalties up to $32,666 per violation, and potential criminal charges. VIP TFRs are particularly strictly enforced by law enforcement and military intercept. Always check TFRs before every flight, even if you flew the same location the previous day.

The Washington, D.C. Special Flight Rules Area (SFRA)

The airspace around Washington, D.C. has unique, permanent restrictions that go beyond standard airspace classifications. The DC SFRA extends approximately 30 NM from the DCA VOR/DME (Reagan National Airport) and requires specific procedures, equipment, and training for all pilots. Within the SFRA is the Flight Restricted Zone (FRZ), which extends approximately 15 NM from DCA and prohibits virtually all general aviation and drone operations.

Part 107 operations within the DC SFRA/FRZ require specific authorization that goes beyond standard LAANC or DroneZone requests. The FAA has established special procedures for drone operations in this area, and violations are treated as potential security threats.

Putting It All Together: Pre-Flight Airspace Checklist

Before every Part 107 flight, use this mental checklist to ensure you are operating legally:

  1. Identify your airspace class: Use a sectional chart or LAANC app to determine the class of airspace at your intended flight location
  2. Determine if authorization is needed: Class B, C, D, and surface E require ATC authorization. Class G does not.
  3. Obtain authorization if required: Use LAANC for near real-time approval or DroneZone for manual requests
  4. Check for TFRs: Use tfr.faa.gov, B4UFLY, or a LAANC app to check for temporary restrictions
  5. Check NOTAMs: Review active NOTAMs for your area for any changes to airspace, obstacles, or procedures
  6. Check for Special Use Airspace: Verify you are not within or near Prohibited, Restricted, or active MOA areas
  7. Verify altitude limits: Confirm the maximum authorized altitude for your grid cell if flying under LAANC authorization
Best Practice: Even experienced remote pilots should run through this checklist before every flight. Airspace conditions can change daily due to TFRs, NOTAMs, and schedule changes at airports. What was Class G yesterday could be under a TFR today.
