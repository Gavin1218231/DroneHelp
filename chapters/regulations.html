Regulations

The foundational regulatory framework for commercial sUAS operations under 14 CFR Part 107, including certification, operating limitations, waivers, and reporting requirements.

Chapter 1: Part 107 Regulations

Welcome to the first chapter of the Part 107 Exam Study Guide. In this chapter we will cover the regulatory framework that governs commercial drone operations in the United States.

  • What is Part 107 and who does it apply to?
  • Remote Pilot Certificate requirements
  • Operating limitations (altitude, speed, visibility, time of day)
  • Weight limits and FAA registration
  • Visual line of sight and crew roles
  • Right of way, operations over people, and moving vehicles
  • Waivers, accident reporting, Remote ID, and recurrent training
Key Point: Part 107 is the primary regulation governing commercial sUAS operations for aircraft weighing less than 55 pounds.

What is Part 107?

Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) Part 107 — officially titled Small Unmanned Aircraft Systems — establishes the rules for non-recreational sUAS operations in the United States National Airspace System.

  • Enacted on August 29, 2016 by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
  • Applies to all commercial, governmental, and other non-recreational drone flights
  • Covers small unmanned aircraft weighing less than 55 lbs (including payload)
  • Replaced the need for individual Section 333 exemptions
Example: A real estate photographer using a drone to capture aerial photos of a property for sale is conducting a commercial operation and must comply with Part 107.
Important: Recreational flyers operating under the Exception for Limited Recreational Operations (44809) are NOT subject to Part 107, but must follow separate rules.

Remote Pilot Certificate Requirements

To operate a small UAS commercially under Part 107, the remote pilot in command (PIC) must hold a Remote Pilot Certificate with an sUAS rating (14 CFR 107.12).

  • Minimum age: 16 years old
  • Knowledge test: Pass the FAA Unmanned Aircraft General (UAG) initial knowledge test at an FAA-approved testing center
  • TSA vetting: Undergo a Transportation Security Administration (TSA) background check
  • English proficiency: Must be able to read, speak, write, and understand the English language
  • Physical/mental fitness: Must be in a physical and mental condition to safely exercise remote PIC privileges
Key Point: Unlike manned aircraft pilots, remote pilots are NOT required to hold an FAA medical certificate. However, a person who holds a Part 61 pilot certificate with a current flight review may take an online recurrent training course instead of the full knowledge test.

Operating Limitations: Altitude & Speed

Part 107 sets strict altitude and speed limitations to keep sUAS safely separated from manned aircraft (14 CFR 107.51).

  • Maximum altitude: 400 feet above ground level (AGL)
  • Structure exception: If operating within a 400-foot radius of a structure, you may fly up to 400 feet above the top of that structure
  • Maximum ground speed: 87 knots (100 mph)
Example: A 200-foot tall radio tower stands in an open field. A remote pilot may legally fly up to 600 feet AGL (400 ft above the 200-ft structure) as long as the drone remains within a 400-foot radius of the tower.
Warning: The 400-foot AGL limit is measured from the ground directly below the drone, not from the launch site. Be careful when flying near hills or terrain changes.

Operating Limitations: Visibility & Cloud Clearance

Minimum weather visibility and cloud clearance requirements help ensure safe separation from manned aircraft and maintain the remote pilot's ability to see and avoid hazards (14 CFR 107.51).

  • Minimum flight visibility: 3 statute miles from the control station
  • Minimum distance below clouds: 500 feet
  • Minimum horizontal distance from clouds: 2,000 feet
Key Point: Flight visibility is the average forward horizontal distance from the control station at which prominent unlighted objects can be seen and identified by day, and prominent lighted objects can be seen and identified by night.
Warning: These are minimum requirements. If conditions deteriorate during a flight, the remote PIC must land the aircraft or adjust operations to maintain compliance.

Operating Limitations: Time of Day

Part 107 restricts when sUAS operations may take place (14 CFR 107.29).

  • Daylight operations: 30 minutes before official sunrise to 30 minutes after official sunset (local time)
  • Civil twilight operations: Permitted during civil twilight with proper anti-collision lighting
  • Anti-collision lighting: Must be visible for at least 3 statute miles and have a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision
  • Night operations: As of the 2021 rule update, night operations are permitted with anti-collision lighting visible for 3 statute miles
Key Point: The January 2021 Operations Over People final rule eliminated the need for a waiver to fly at night. Remote pilots must complete updated training or recurrent training that includes night operations knowledge areas.
Example: If official sunset is at 7:00 PM, a remote pilot may continue flying until 7:30 PM under daylight rules. After 7:30 PM, anti-collision lighting visible for 3 statute miles is required.

Weight Limits & FAA Registration

Part 107 applies only to small unmanned aircraft within a specific weight range. Registration with the FAA is also required (14 CFR 107.3, 48.100).

  • Maximum takeoff weight: Less than 55 lbs (25 kg), including everything onboard or attached at the time of takeoff
  • Registration required: All sUAS weighing between 0.55 lbs (250 grams) and 55 lbs must be registered with the FAA
  • Registration fee: $5 per aircraft (valid for 3 years)
  • Registration marking: Registration number must be displayed on the exterior of the aircraft or accessible without tools in a compartment
Important: The 55-lb limit includes payload and any attached equipment such as cameras, sensors, or delivery packages. Exceeding this weight requires a different type of airworthiness certification.

Visual Line of Sight & Crew Requirements

Maintaining visual line of sight (VLOS) is one of the most fundamental requirements of Part 107 (14 CFR 107.31, 107.33, 107.35).

  • VLOS required: The remote PIC or visual observer must be able to see the drone unaided (except for corrective lenses) at all times during flight
  • Visual observer (VO): Optional, not required; assists with see-and-avoid duties when used
  • One pilot per drone: A remote PIC may only operate one sUAS at a time
  • No operations from moving aircraft: You cannot operate an sUAS from a manned aircraft in flight
  • Communication: Remote PIC and VO must maintain effective communication at all times
Key Point: First-person view (FPV) goggles alone do NOT satisfy the VLOS requirement. A visual observer or the PIC's unaided vision must be used to maintain line of sight.

Right of Way, Moving Vehicles & Operations Over People

Part 107 addresses the right-of-way hierarchy, operations from moving vehicles, and the rules for flying over people (14 CFR 107.37, 107.25, Subpart D).

  • Right of way: sUAS must yield the right of way to all manned aircraft at all times
  • Moving vehicles: Operating from a moving vehicle is only permitted in sparsely populated areas (not over people not directly participating)
  • Operations over people categories:
    • Category 1: Aircraft weighing 0.55 lbs (250 g) or less, including everything onboard
    • Category 2: Must not cause injury above a defined severity threshold; no exposed rotating parts
    • Category 3: Same injury limit as Category 2 but cannot operate over open-air assemblies; must not sustain flight over people unless in a restricted area
    • Category 4: Requires airworthiness certificate, operating limitations, and maintenance/inspection per manufacturer program
Important: Operating from a moving vehicle in a populated area requires a Part 107 waiver. The remote PIC must always maintain VLOS regardless of whether the vehicle is moving.

Waivers & Accident Reporting

The FAA allows Part 107 operators to request waivers for certain rules, and mandates accident reporting under specific conditions (14 CFR 107.200, 107.9).

  • Waivers: Submitted through the FAA DroneZone website for operations outside standard Part 107 rules
  • Common waiverable provisions: Operations over people (prior to 2021 rule), beyond visual line of sight (BVLOS), from a moving vehicle in populated areas, multiple aircraft by one pilot
  • Waiver requirements: Applicant must demonstrate equivalent level of safety

Accident Reporting (14 CFR 107.9)

  • Timeframe: Must report to the FAA within 10 calendar days
  • Reportable events:
    • Serious injury to any person
    • Loss of consciousness of any person
    • Damage to any property (other than the sUAS) of at least $500
Key Point: Damage to the drone itself does not count toward the $500 property damage reporting threshold. Only damage to other property triggers the reporting requirement.

Remote Identification (Remote ID)

Remote ID is the ability of a drone in flight to provide identification and location information that can be received by other parties. As of September 16, 2023, Remote ID compliance is required (14 CFR Part 89).

  • Standard Remote ID: Built into the drone by the manufacturer; broadcasts drone ID, location, altitude, velocity, control station location, and emergency status
  • Remote ID Broadcast Module: An add-on device for drones without built-in Remote ID; broadcasts from the takeoff location instead of control station location
  • FAA-Recognized Identification Area (FRIA): A defined geographic area where drones without Remote ID may operate; established by community-based organizations (CBOs) or educational institutions
Important: Operating without Remote ID compliance outside of a FRIA is a violation of federal regulations and can result in enforcement action.
Key Point: Remote ID does not require an internet connection to function. It broadcasts identification information using radio frequency broadcast technology (typically Wi-Fi or Bluetooth).

Recurrent Training & Certificate Renewal

Remote Pilot Certificates do not expire, but pilots must complete recurrent training to maintain their privileges (14 CFR 107.65).

  • Recurrent training interval: Every 24 calendar months from the date of initial knowledge test or last recurrent training/test
  • Online option: Recurrent training is available as a free online course through the FAA Safety (FAASTeam) website
  • Knowledge areas refreshed:
    • Applicable regulations
    • Airspace classification and operating requirements
    • Aviation weather sources and effects on sUAS performance
    • sUAS loading and performance
    • Emergency procedures and crew resource management
    • Operations at night (added with 2021 rule update)
  • Alternative: Pilots can retake the proctored knowledge test instead of completing online training
Key Point: If a remote pilot's recurrent training lapses, they cannot exercise the privileges of their certificate until they complete the recurrent training. The certificate itself remains valid but the pilot is not authorized to fly commercially.

IACRA Application Process

After passing the UAG knowledge test at an FAA-approved testing center, the next step is to apply for your Remote Pilot Certificate through IACRA (Integrated Airman Certification and Rating Application).

  • Create an IACRA account: Visit the IACRA website and register for an account if you do not already have one
  • Start a new application: Select "Remote Pilot Certificate" as the application type and follow the prompts to enter your knowledge test ID and personal information
  • TSA background check: A Transportation Security Administration (TSA) background check is triggered automatically when your application is submitted — no separate action is required on your part
  • Temporary certificate: Once the TSA vetting is complete (typically within a few days), a temporary certificate is issued that allows you to begin exercising remote pilot privileges immediately
  • Permanent certificate: Your permanent plastic Remote Pilot Certificate card arrives by mail in approximately 6–8 weeks
  • Identity verification: You must have a valid government-issued photo ID available for identity verification throughout the application process
Key Point: Your temporary certificate is just as valid as the permanent card. Keep it accessible during all flight operations until your permanent certificate arrives.

Part 61 Pilot Pathway

Holders of an existing Part 61 pilot certificate have an alternative, streamlined pathway to obtain a Remote Pilot Certificate without taking the proctored knowledge test.

  • Eligibility: You must hold a Part 61 pilot certificate other than a student pilot certificate (e.g., private, commercial, or ATP certificate)
  • Current flight review required: You must have completed a flight review within the preceding 24 calendar months, as required under 14 CFR 61.56
  • Online training course: Instead of the proctored UAG knowledge test, eligible Part 61 pilots complete a free online training course (Part 107 small UAS Initial Training) available through the FAA Safety Team (FAASTeam) website
  • IACRA application still required: After completing the online training, you must still apply through IACRA for the Remote Pilot Certificate
  • TSA vetting still required: The TSA background check is still triggered and must be cleared before the certificate is issued
Key Point: The Part 61 pathway saves time and money by replacing the proctored test with an online course, but the IACRA application and TSA vetting steps remain the same as for non-pilot applicants.

FAA Enforcement Actions & Penalties

The FAA has a range of enforcement tools to address violations of Part 107 and other federal aviation regulations. Understanding the enforcement process is important for every remote pilot.

  • Warning letters: Informal notices for minor, unintentional violations — no formal action taken
  • Letters of Correction: Acknowledgment of a violation with corrective action required; no fine or certificate action
  • Civil penalties: Monetary fines up to $32,666 per violation for individuals under 49 USC §46301
  • Certificate actions: Suspension or revocation of the Remote Pilot Certificate for serious or repeated violations
  • Criminal penalties: Possible for knowingly or recklessly endangering aircraft operations or persons

Enforcement Process

  • Investigation → Notice of Proposed Action → Informal Conference → Formal Hearing (if unresolved)
Key Point: Most first-time unintentional violations result in counseling, warning letters, or letters of correction rather than civil penalties or certificate actions.

Waiver Application Process

When an operation requires deviation from standard Part 107 rules, remote pilots can apply for a waiver through the FAA DroneZone portal (dronezone.faa.gov).

  • Application portal: FAA DroneZone at dronezone.faa.gov
  • Key waiverable provisions: Operations over people beyond Cat 1–4, beyond visual line of sight, altitude above 400 ft, multiple aircraft by one pilot, from moving vehicle in populated area, yield right of way
  • No longer needs waiver: Night operations (§107.29) — removed in the 2021 final rule
  • Not waiverable: Registration, airworthiness, drug/alcohol rules, Remote PIC certificate requirement
  • Application requires: Detailed safety case and risk mitigation plan demonstrating equivalent level of safety
  • Typical processing time: Approximately 90 days — plan well in advance
Important: Not all Part 107 rules are waiverable. Registration requirements, airworthiness standards, drug and alcohol prohibitions, and the Remote PIC certificate requirement cannot be waived under any circumstances.

Post-Certification Compliance & Record-Keeping

After earning your Remote Pilot Certificate, ongoing compliance obligations ensure you remain authorized to conduct commercial sUAS operations.

  • Certificate validity: The Remote Pilot Certificate does not expire, but you must complete recurrent training every 24 calendar months to exercise its privileges
  • Making certificate available: Must be available for inspection upon request (physical card or digital copy via IACRA)
  • Change of address: Must notify the FAA within 30 days of a permanent address change
  • Change of name: Must apply for an updated certificate reflecting the new legal name
  • Voluntary surrender: A pilot may voluntarily surrender their certificate at any time
  • Involuntary revocation: The FAA may revoke a certificate for serious violations or failure to meet eligibility requirements
Key Point: Failure to complete recurrent training does not revoke your certificate — it simply means you cannot exercise its privileges until you complete the required training.

In-Depth Reading: Part 107 Regulations

Introduction to 14 CFR Part 107

Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 107 (14 CFR Part 107), titled Small Unmanned Aircraft Systems, is the foundational regulation governing commercial and other non-recreational drone operations in the United States. Published as a final rule by the Federal Aviation Administration on June 28, 2016, and effective on August 29, 2016, Part 107 replaced the previously cumbersome Section 333 exemption process and opened the door for widespread legal commercial drone use. Since its initial adoption, Part 107 has been updated several times, most notably in January 2021 with the Operations Over People and Night Operations final rule, and in 2023 with Remote Identification requirements.

Part 107 applies to the operation of small unmanned aircraft systems (sUAS) in the National Airspace System (NAS). A "small unmanned aircraft" is defined as any unmanned aircraft weighing less than 55 pounds on takeoff, including everything that is on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft at the time of takeoff. This regulation covers a broad range of commercial activities, from aerial photography and videography to agricultural surveying, infrastructure inspection, delivery services, search and rescue support, and much more.

Key Terminology: Throughout Part 107, the term "small unmanned aircraft system" (sUAS) refers to the entire system, including the drone itself, its control station, any communication links, and any other associated equipment. The term "small unmanned aircraft" refers specifically to the flying vehicle. Understanding this distinction is important for the knowledge test.

Remote Pilot Certificate (14 CFR 107.12, 107.61, 107.63)

The cornerstone of legal commercial sUAS operation is the Remote Pilot Certificate with a Small UAS Rating. Any person acting as the remote pilot in command (PIC) of a small UAS for purposes other than recreation must hold this certificate. The certificate is issued by the FAA after the applicant meets the following requirements:

  • Age requirement (107.61(a)): The applicant must be at least 16 years of age. There is no maximum age limit.
  • English language proficiency: The applicant must be able to read, speak, write, and understand the English language. Exceptions may be made if the person is unable to meet one of these requirements for a medical reason, in which case limitations may be placed on the certificate.
  • Physical and mental fitness: The applicant must be in a physical and mental condition that allows safe exercise of remote PIC privileges. Notably, Part 107 does NOT require a medical certificate, unlike manned aircraft operations under Part 61. However, a remote pilot must self-certify their fitness and must not operate if they know or have reason to know of any physical or mental condition that would interfere with the safe operation of the sUAS.
  • Knowledge test: The applicant must pass the Unmanned Aircraft General (UAG) initial knowledge examination at an FAA-approved Knowledge Testing Center. The test consists of 60 multiple-choice questions, and a score of 70% or higher is required to pass. The test covers airspace classification, operating requirements, weather, loading and performance, emergency procedures, crew resource management, radio communication, physiological factors, aeronautical decision-making, maintenance, and applicable regulations.
  • TSA security vetting: After passing the knowledge test, the applicant must apply for the Remote Pilot Certificate through the FAA Integrated Airman Certification and Rating Application (IACRA) system. The application triggers a background check by the Transportation Security Administration (TSA). The certificate is typically issued within a few weeks.
Study Tip: Holders of a Part 61 pilot certificate (other than student pilot) with a current flight review within the preceding 24 months may obtain their initial Remote Pilot Certificate by completing an FAA online training course instead of taking the proctored knowledge test at a testing center. This alternative path is defined in 14 CFR 107.63.

Operating Limitations: Altitude, Speed, Visibility, and Cloud Clearance (14 CFR 107.51)

Section 107.51 establishes the core operating limitations that define where and how a small UAS may be flown. These limitations exist primarily to maintain safe separation between sUAS operations and manned aircraft traffic, and to ensure the remote PIC maintains adequate situational awareness.

Altitude

The maximum allowable altitude for sUAS operations under Part 107 is 400 feet above ground level (AGL). This altitude is measured from the ground directly beneath the aircraft, not from the takeoff point. This means that when flying over terrain that varies in elevation, the pilot must account for changes in ground level to remain within the 400-foot AGL limit.

There is one important exception: if the small UAS is operating within a 400-foot radius of a structure, it may fly up to 400 feet above the top of that structure. For example, if a building is 300 feet tall, the pilot may operate up to 700 feet AGL (300-foot structure + 400 feet above it) as long as the drone stays within a 400-foot lateral radius of the structure. This exception exists to allow inspections and other operations near tall structures.

Speed

The maximum groundspeed for a small UAS under Part 107 is 87 knots, which is equivalent to 100 miles per hour. This limitation applies to ground speed, not airspeed. In practice, most consumer and commercial drones operate well below this speed limit, but it is important to be aware of it, especially when operating in high-wind conditions where a tailwind could push the drone's ground speed higher than expected.

Visibility

The minimum flight visibility from the control station must be at least 3 statute miles. Flight visibility is defined as the average forward horizontal distance from the cockpit (in this case, the control station) at which prominent unlighted objects can be seen and identified by day, and prominent lighted objects can be seen and identified at night. The remote PIC is responsible for determining the flight visibility. If conditions deteriorate below 3 statute miles during a flight, the remote PIC must land the aircraft or adjust operations.

Cloud Clearance

Part 107 requires the small UAS to maintain a minimum distance of 500 feet below clouds vertically and 2,000 feet from clouds horizontally. These cloud clearance requirements are designed to keep drones well separated from manned aircraft that may be operating in or near clouds. Remember that manned VFR aircraft typically require 500 feet below, 1,000 feet above, and 2,000 feet horizontal separation from clouds in Class E airspace, so these requirements help maintain consistent separation standards.

Exam Alert: The visibility and cloud clearance requirements under Part 107 are among the most frequently tested topics on the knowledge exam. Memorize: 3 statute miles visibility, 500 feet below clouds, 2,000 feet horizontal from clouds. These cannot be waived.

Time of Day and Lighting Requirements (14 CFR 107.29)

Under the original 2016 Part 107 rule, sUAS operations were limited to daylight hours and civil twilight with anti-collision lighting. The January 2021 Operations Over People final rule significantly expanded this by allowing routine night operations without a waiver, subject to specific conditions.

The current rule states that small UAS may be operated:

  1. During daylight: From 30 minutes before official sunrise to 30 minutes after official sunset, local time.
  2. During civil twilight and at night: With anti-collision lighting that is visible for at least 3 statute miles and has a sufficient flash rate to avoid collision. The remote PIC may reduce the intensity of the anti-collision light if it creates a safety hazard (for example, by blinding the pilot).

To exercise night operations privileges, the remote PIC must have completed initial knowledge testing or recurrent training that includes the updated night operations content. Pilots who obtained their certificate before the 2021 rule change must complete updated recurrent training before flying at night.

Definition — Civil Twilight: Civil twilight begins in the morning when the center of the sun is 6 degrees below the horizon and ends at sunrise. In the evening, it begins at sunset and ends when the center of the sun reaches 6 degrees below the horizon. During civil twilight, there is generally enough natural light to see objects on the ground, but artificial lighting may be necessary for precise operations.

Weight Limits and FAA Registration (14 CFR 107.3, 14 CFR Part 48)

Part 107 governs the operation of small unmanned aircraft weighing less than 55 pounds (25 kilograms) at takeoff, including everything that is on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft. This weight includes the aircraft frame, batteries, cameras, sensors, payload, and any other attached equipment.

FAA registration is required for all unmanned aircraft weighing more than 0.55 pounds (250 grams) and less than 55 pounds. Registration is completed online through the FAA DroneZone website. For Part 107 commercial operators, each individual aircraft must be registered under Part 48, which costs $5 per aircraft and is valid for 3 years. The registration number must be displayed on the exterior surface of the aircraft, or it may be placed in an interior compartment accessible without the use of tools if the aircraft has Remote ID capability.

Practical Note: Recreational operators may register under Part 48 using a single registration number for all of their aircraft ($5 total), but Part 107 commercial operators must register each drone individually ($5 per drone). Make sure you understand this distinction for the exam.

Visual Line of Sight (14 CFR 107.31)

One of the most fundamental requirements of Part 107 is that the remote PIC or the visual observer must maintain visual line of sight (VLOS) with the small unmanned aircraft throughout the entire flight. VLOS means the ability to see the aircraft with unaided vision (other than corrective lenses such as glasses or contact lenses). Binoculars, telescopes, onboard cameras, and first-person view (FPV) systems do NOT satisfy the VLOS requirement.

The purpose of the VLOS requirement is to enable the remote PIC (or VO) to know the drone's location, determine its attitude and direction of flight, observe the airspace for traffic and other hazards, and determine that the aircraft does not endanger the life or property of others. If visual line of sight is ever lost, the remote PIC must take immediate action to regain VLOS or safely land the aircraft.

Crew Roles: Remote PIC, Visual Observer, and Other Crew (14 CFR 107.33, 107.35)

Part 107 defines several crew roles for sUAS operations:

  • Remote Pilot in Command (PIC) (107.19): The person who has final authority and responsibility for the operation and safety of the sUAS flight. The remote PIC must hold a valid Remote Pilot Certificate. Only one person may serve as remote PIC at any given time, and a remote PIC may only control one sUAS at a time (unless a waiver is obtained).
  • Person Manipulating the Controls (107.35): A person other than the remote PIC may manipulate the flight controls of the sUAS under the direct supervision of the remote PIC. This person does not need to hold a Remote Pilot Certificate, but they must be briefed on the operating conditions, emergency procedures, and their responsibilities. The remote PIC retains full responsibility for the flight.
  • Visual Observer (VO) (107.33): A visual observer is an optional crew member who assists the remote PIC with scanning the airspace and maintaining visual contact with the sUAS. The VO must be able to communicate directly with the remote PIC at all times. The VO does not need a Remote Pilot Certificate. When a VO is used, the remote PIC does not need to personally maintain VLOS, but either the PIC or the VO must maintain VLOS at all times.
Exam Alert: The exam may test you on the relationship between the remote PIC, VO, and person manipulating the controls. Remember: only the remote PIC needs a certificate, the VO is optional (not required), and the remote PIC always retains final responsibility regardless of who else is involved.

Operations from Moving Vehicles (14 CFR 107.25)

Under Part 107, operating a small UAS from a moving land or water vehicle is permitted only in sparsely populated areas. The intent of this restriction is to minimize risk to people on the ground. In a sparsely populated area, the remote PIC or VO may operate from a moving vehicle as long as all other Part 107 requirements are met, including VLOS. Operating from a moving aircraft is prohibited entirely.

If an operator wants to fly from a moving vehicle in a populated area, they must obtain a waiver from the FAA. The waiver application must demonstrate that the operation can be conducted safely and that appropriate risk mitigations are in place.

Right of Way (14 CFR 107.37)

Small unmanned aircraft must yield the right of way to all other aircraft, manned and unmanned. This is an absolute requirement with no exceptions. The remote PIC must remain vigilant and be prepared to take immediate evasive action if another aircraft is observed in the vicinity. This means altering course, altitude, or landing the drone to avoid any potential conflict. Yielding right of way is not optional — it is a regulatory mandate that reflects the principle that manned aircraft, carrying human passengers and crew, have priority in the NAS.

Operations Over People (14 CFR Part 107, Subpart D)

The January 2021 final rule established four categories for operations over people, allowing appropriately equipped drones to fly over non-participating persons without a waiver:

  1. Category 1: The small unmanned aircraft (including everything attached) weighs 0.55 pounds (250 grams) or less. No additional requirements beyond standard Part 107 rules. No FAA-accepted declaration of compliance needed.
  2. Category 2: The aircraft must not cause injury to a person that exceeds a defined severity threshold based on impact kinetic energy. The aircraft must not have any exposed rotating parts that could lacerate human skin. The manufacturer must submit a Declaration of Compliance (DOC) to the FAA, and the FAA must accept it.
  3. Category 3: Same injury severity threshold as Category 2, and no exposed rotating parts that could lacerate skin. However, Category 3 operations have additional restrictions: the aircraft may not be operated over open-air assemblies of people, and the aircraft must not sustain flight over any person unless that person is under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle. Transitional flight over people is permitted.
  4. Category 4: Requires an FAA-issued airworthiness certificate. The aircraft must be operated in accordance with the operating limitations specified by the FAA. The operator must comply with all manufacturer maintenance and inspection requirements.
Important Distinction: "Operations over people" applies to non-participating persons. People who are directly participating in the sUAS operation (the remote PIC, VO, and other crew members) are excluded from this definition. Additionally, persons under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle are not considered to be persons that the drone is operating "over."

Waivers and Airspace Authorizations (14 CFR 107.200)

The FAA recognizes that certain operations may need to deviate from standard Part 107 rules. To accommodate this, the FAA allows operators to apply for waivers through the FAA DroneZone portal. A waiver allows the certificate holder to deviate from specific provisions of Part 107 if they can demonstrate that the proposed operation can be conducted safely under the terms of the waiver.

Provisions that may be waived include:

  • 107.25 — Operation from a moving vehicle or aircraft
  • 107.29 — Daylight operation (less relevant since the 2021 night operations rule)
  • 107.31 — Visual line of sight requirement (BVLOS)
  • 107.33 — Visual observer
  • 107.35 — Operation of multiple sUAS
  • 107.37(a) — Yielding the right of way
  • 107.39 — Operations over people (less relevant since the 2021 rule for Categories 1-4)
  • 107.41 — Operations in certain airspace
  • 107.51 — Operating limitations (altitude, speed, visibility, cloud clearance)

The waiver application must include a detailed description of the proposed operation, a risk assessment, and the mitigation strategies that will be employed. The FAA typically takes 90 days or more to process waiver requests, so operators should plan well in advance. Approved waivers are published on the FAA website for public access.

Study Tip: Not all provisions of Part 107 can be waived. For example, the requirement to register the drone, the requirement for the remote PIC to hold a certificate, and the accident reporting requirements cannot be waived.

Accident Reporting (14 CFR 107.9)

The remote PIC must report any sUAS accident to the FAA within 10 calendar days if the accident results in any of the following:

  • Serious injury to any person (defined as an injury that requires hospitalization, results in a broken bone, causes burns, involves significant blood loss, or affects any internal organ)
  • Any loss of consciousness by any person
  • Damage to any property, other than the small unmanned aircraft, of at least $500 to repair or replace the property at fair market value

Reports are filed through the FAA DroneZone portal. It is critical to note that damage to the drone itself does not count toward the $500 property damage threshold. Only damage to third-party property triggers this reporting requirement. Additionally, the $500 threshold is based on the cost to repair or fair market replacement value, whichever is lower.

Exam Alert: The accident reporting timeframe (10 calendar days) and the $500 property damage threshold (excluding damage to the drone itself) are frequently tested on the knowledge exam. Make sure you memorize these values.

Remote Identification (14 CFR Part 89)

Remote Identification (Remote ID) is a system that allows drones to broadcast identification and location information during flight. Think of it as a "digital license plate" for drones. The Remote ID rule (14 CFR Part 89) was finalized in January 2021 and the operational compliance date was September 16, 2023. Since that date, all drones operating in US airspace must comply with Remote ID requirements.

There are three ways to comply with Remote ID:

  1. Standard Remote ID: The drone has built-in Remote ID capability as designed by the manufacturer. It broadcasts the drone's identity (serial number or session ID), its latitude, longitude, and altitude, its velocity, the control station's latitude, longitude, and altitude, a time mark, and an emergency status indication. This information is broadcast via radio frequency (typically Bluetooth or Wi-Fi) and does not require an internet connection.
  2. Remote ID Broadcast Module: A separate hardware device attached to a drone that does not have built-in Remote ID. The broadcast module transmits similar information to Standard Remote ID, except it broadcasts the takeoff location instead of the control station location. The drone must remain within visual line of sight of the pilot when using a broadcast module.
  3. FAA-Recognized Identification Area (FRIA): A defined geographic area where drones without any form of Remote ID may operate. FRIAs are established by FAA-recognized community-based organizations (CBOs) or educational institutions. Drones operating within a FRIA must remain within the boundaries of the area and below 400 feet AGL.
Why Remote ID Matters: Remote ID serves multiple purposes: it helps the FAA, law enforcement, and other federal agencies identify drones operating in the airspace, it supports airspace management and safety, and it is considered a critical enabling technology for more advanced operations such as beyond visual line of sight (BVLOS) flights and drone deliveries in urban environments.

Recurrent Training and Certificate Maintenance (14 CFR 107.65)

The Remote Pilot Certificate does not expire, but the pilot's authorization to exercise the privileges of the certificate does lapse if recurrent training is not completed on time. Under 14 CFR 107.65, a remote pilot must complete one of the following every 24 calendar months:

  • Complete an online recurrent training course through the FAA Safety Team (FAASTeam) website at no cost, or
  • Pass the recurrent knowledge test (UAR) at an FAA-approved testing center

The 24-month period is calculated from the month the pilot last completed either an initial knowledge test, a recurrent knowledge test, or an online recurrent training course. For example, if a pilot passes their initial knowledge test in March 2024, they must complete recurrent training by the end of March 2026.

The recurrent training covers the following knowledge areas:

  • Applicable regulations relating to sUAS privileges, limitations, and flight operation
  • Airspace classification, operating requirements, and flight restrictions
  • Aviation weather sources and effects of weather on small unmanned aircraft performance
  • Small unmanned aircraft loading and performance
  • Emergency procedures
  • Crew resource management
  • Radio communication procedures
  • Determining the performance of small unmanned aircraft
  • Physiological effects of drugs and alcohol
  • Aeronautical decision-making and judgment
  • Airport operations and traffic patterns
  • Night operations (added in 2021)

If a remote pilot allows their recurrent training to lapse, their certificate remains valid but they may not exercise the privileges of that certificate until they complete the required training. There is no penalty for letting the training lapse — the pilot simply needs to complete the training before resuming commercial operations.

Practical Advice: Set a calendar reminder for your recurrent training deadline. The FAASTeam online course is free and can typically be completed in about one to two hours. Do not wait until the last minute, as system outages or technical issues could prevent you from completing the course on time.

The Application Process: IACRA and TSA Vetting

Once you have passed the Unmanned Aircraft General (UAG) knowledge test at an FAA-approved testing center, the certification process continues through IACRA (Integrated Airman Certification and Rating Application), the FAA's online airman certification system. To begin, create an account on the IACRA website if you do not already have one, then log in and start a new application. Select "Remote Pilot Certificate — Small UAS Rating" as the certificate type. You will be prompted to enter personal information, your FAA Tracking Number (FTN), and the knowledge test ID from your exam results. After reviewing all information for accuracy, submit the application. A valid government-issued photo ID is required for identity verification, and a certified flight instructor (CFI), designated pilot examiner (DPE), or FAA official must validate your identity before the application can be finalized.

Upon submission of your IACRA application, a TSA security background check is automatically initiated. This vetting process typically takes anywhere from a few days to several weeks, depending on individual circumstances. During this period, you can monitor the status of your application through your IACRA account. Once TSA vetting is complete and no disqualifying issues are found, a temporary Remote Pilot Certificate is generated and made available for download through IACRA. This temporary certificate carries the same legal authority as the permanent certificate and allows you to begin conducting commercial sUAS operations immediately. The permanent plastic certificate card is mailed to the address on file and typically arrives within 6 to 8 weeks after issuance.

For applicants who already hold a Part 61 pilot certificate (other than a student pilot certificate) with a current flight review completed within the preceding 24 calendar months, an alternative pathway is available. Instead of sitting for the proctored UAG knowledge test, these pilots may complete the Part 107 small UAS Initial Training course online through the FAA Safety Team (FAASTeam) website at no cost. This online course covers the same knowledge areas as the proctored exam but is self-paced and can be completed from any location with internet access. After finishing the online training, Part 61 pilots must still apply through IACRA and undergo TSA vetting, following the same steps described above. This streamlined pathway recognizes the existing aeronautical knowledge of certificated pilots while ensuring they understand the specific regulations and operational requirements unique to small UAS operations under Part 107.

Practical Tip: After receiving your temporary certificate through IACRA, print a copy and keep it with you during all drone operations. Many commercial clients and law enforcement officers may ask to see your certificate on-site. A digital copy saved on your phone or tablet is also recommended as a backup. Remember that you cannot legally exercise remote pilot privileges until the temporary or permanent certificate has been issued, even if you have already passed the knowledge test.

Additional Regulatory Considerations

Drug and Alcohol Use (14 CFR 107.27)

Part 107 incorporates the same drug and alcohol provisions as other parts of the FARs. Specifically, no person may operate or serve as a crew member of a small UAS within 8 hours of consuming any alcoholic beverage, while under the influence of alcohol, while using any drug that affects the person's faculties in any way contrary to safety, or while having a blood alcohol content of 0.04% or greater. Refusal to submit to a drug or alcohol test is grounds for certificate denial, suspension, or revocation.

Preflight Inspection (14 CFR 107.49)

Before each flight, the remote PIC must inspect the small UAS to ensure it is in a condition for safe operation. This includes checking the communications links between the control station and the aircraft, and ensuring that there is sufficient power for the intended operation. The specific elements of the preflight inspection should follow the manufacturer's recommendations and any additional procedures established by the operator.

Hazardous Operations (14 CFR 107.23)

No person may operate a small UAS in a careless or reckless manner that would endanger the life or property of another. Additionally, no person may allow an object to be dropped from a small UAS in a manner that creates an undue hazard to persons or property. Note that this does not prohibit dropping objects altogether — only drops that create undue hazard. Planned cargo drops or agricultural applications may be conducted as long as appropriate safety measures are in place.

Airspace Authorizations (14 CFR 107.41)

Operations in controlled airspace (Class B, C, D, or the lateral boundaries of the surface area of Class E airspace designated for an airport) require prior authorization from the FAA. This authorization can be obtained through the LAANC (Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability) system for near-real-time approvals, or through the FAA DroneZone portal for more complex requests. Operations in uncontrolled airspace (Class G) generally do not require authorization but are still subject to all other Part 107 rules.

LAANC System: The Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability (LAANC) is an automated system that provides near-real-time processing of airspace authorization requests in controlled airspace near airports. LAANC uses UAS Facility Maps that define the maximum altitudes at which drones can operate in specific grid cells around airports. If your requested altitude is at or below the published ceiling, LAANC can provide authorization in seconds. This is covered in more detail in Chapter 2: Airspace.

FAA Enforcement Actions & Penalties

The FAA has broad authority to enforce compliance with federal aviation regulations, including Part 107. Understanding the range of enforcement tools available to the FAA is important not only for the Part 107 knowledge exam but also for everyday operations as a remote pilot. The FAA's enforcement approach generally follows a progressive philosophy: minor, unintentional violations are typically addressed through informal measures, while serious, willful, or repeated violations can lead to substantial penalties and loss of flying privileges.

The FAA uses several types of enforcement actions, which escalate in severity depending on the nature and circumstances of the violation:

Enforcement Action Description Typical Use
Warning Letter An informal notice advising the pilot of a regulatory violation. No formal record of enforcement action is created. Minor, first-time, unintentional violations where the pilot demonstrates good faith
Letter of Correction Acknowledges the violation and requires the pilot to take specific corrective action (e.g., additional training). No monetary fine is imposed. Low-severity violations where corrective action is sufficient to prevent recurrence
Civil Penalty A monetary fine imposed for regulatory violations. Under 49 USC §46301, civil penalties can reach up to $32,666 per violation for individuals. Moderate to serious violations, repeated offenses, or cases where informal action is insufficient
Certificate Action (Suspension) Temporary suspension of the Remote Pilot Certificate for a defined period, during which the pilot may not exercise certificate privileges. Serious violations involving safety risks, or patterns of non-compliance
Certificate Action (Revocation) Permanent withdrawal of the Remote Pilot Certificate. The pilot must reapply and meet all requirements from scratch to regain certification. The most severe violations, including willful endangerment, fraud, or drug/alcohol offenses
Criminal Penalties Criminal prosecution for knowingly or recklessly operating a drone in a manner that endangers aircraft operations or persons. Intentional interference with manned aircraft, operations near airports that endanger lives

The FAA enforcement process typically follows a structured sequence: it begins with an investigation, which may be triggered by a complaint, an observed violation, or an incident report. If a violation is confirmed, the FAA issues a Notice of Proposed Action detailing the charges and proposed penalty. The certificate holder then has the option to request an informal conference with the FAA to discuss the matter and potentially negotiate a resolution. If the matter cannot be resolved informally, the case proceeds to a formal hearing before an administrative law judge at the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB). The certificate holder may appeal an adverse decision through the NTSB and, ultimately, to a federal court of appeals.

It is worth noting that the FAA generally takes a compliance-oriented approach, particularly for first-time, unintentional violations. The FAA Compliance Program, established in 2015, emphasizes corrective action and education over punitive measures when the certificate holder is willing to cooperate and the violation did not involve willful misconduct or a significant safety risk. For example, a remote pilot who inadvertently flies slightly above 400 feet AGL for the first time and promptly corrects the error is more likely to receive counseling or a warning letter than a civil penalty. However, a pilot who repeatedly ignores altitude restrictions or intentionally flies near an active runway can expect severe enforcement action.

Exam Alert: Know the difference between warning letters, letters of correction, civil penalties, and certificate actions. The exam may present a scenario and ask which enforcement outcome is most likely. Remember that civil penalties for individuals can reach up to $32,666 per violation under 49 USC §46301, and that criminal penalties apply when a person knowingly endangers aircraft operations.
Study Tip: Most first-time unintentional violations by cooperative certificate holders result in counseling, warning letters, or letters of correction rather than civil penalties or certificate actions. The FAA's Compliance Program favors education and corrective action over punishment when safety is not significantly compromised.

Waiver Application Process (14 CFR 107.200)

While Part 107 establishes a clear set of operating rules for commercial sUAS operations, the FAA recognizes that certain legitimate operations may require deviations from these standard rules. The waiver process provides a legal pathway for operators to conduct operations that would otherwise violate specific Part 107 provisions, provided the applicant can demonstrate that the proposed operation can be conducted safely. All waiver applications are submitted through the FAA DroneZone portal at dronezone.faa.gov, the same platform used for drone registration and airspace authorization requests.

Not every Part 107 rule can be waived. The following provisions are commonly waiverable:

  • §107.25: Operation from a moving vehicle or aircraft in populated areas
  • §107.31: Visual line of sight (BVLOS operations)
  • §107.33: Visual observer requirements
  • §107.35: Operation of multiple small UAS by a single pilot
  • §107.37(a): Yielding the right of way
  • §107.39: Operations over people beyond Category 1–4
  • §107.41: Operations in certain airspace
  • §107.51: Operating limitations (altitude above 400 ft, speed, visibility, cloud clearance)

Importantly, the 2021 final rule removed night operations (§107.29) from the list of provisions requiring a waiver. Remote pilots who have completed updated initial or recurrent training may now fly at night without a waiver, provided anti-collision lighting visible for 3 statute miles is used. However, certain rules are never waiverable, including FAA registration requirements, airworthiness standards, the Remote PIC certificate requirement, drug and alcohol prohibitions, and accident reporting obligations.

The waiver application itself requires the operator to build a compelling safety case. This includes a detailed description of the proposed operation, identification of the specific Part 107 provisions to be waived, a thorough risk assessment identifying all hazards associated with the operation, and a comprehensive risk mitigation plan explaining how each hazard will be addressed to achieve an equivalent level of safety. The FAA reviewers evaluate whether the proposed mitigations are adequate and whether the operation can be conducted without posing undue risk to other aircraft, persons, or property on the ground. Applications that are incomplete, vague, or fail to adequately address risk are likely to be denied.

The typical FAA processing time for waiver applications is approximately 90 days, though complex requests (especially BVLOS waivers) may take significantly longer. Operators should plan well in advance and submit applications with ample lead time before the intended operation date. Once approved, waivers are issued with specific terms and conditions that the operator must follow, and approved waivers are published on the FAA website for public transparency. Waivers have defined durations and geographic boundaries, and the operator must reapply when the waiver expires or if the operation parameters change.

Exam Alert: Not all Part 107 rules are waiverable. The exam frequently tests whether students can identify which provisions can and cannot be waived. Remember: registration, airworthiness, drug/alcohol rules, and the Remote PIC certificate requirement are NEVER waiverable. Also remember that night operations no longer require a waiver since the 2021 rule update.
Example: A utility company wants to inspect a 600-foot transmission tower using a drone. Since this requires exceeding the 400-foot AGL altitude limit, the company must apply for a waiver of §107.51 through FAA DroneZone. Their application would include details about the inspection procedure, the specific altitude requested, the safety equipment and procedures that will be used (e.g., NOTAM issuance, visual observers, coordination with local air traffic), and an explanation of how the operation will maintain an equivalent level of safety. They should submit the application at least 90 days before the planned inspection date.

Post-Certification Compliance & Record-Keeping

Earning a Remote Pilot Certificate is an important milestone, but it is only the beginning of a remote pilot's ongoing compliance obligations. The FAA requires certificate holders to maintain certain records, keep their personal information current, and complete periodic training to retain the privilege of conducting commercial sUAS operations. Understanding these post-certification requirements is essential for the Part 107 knowledge exam and for maintaining good standing as a remote pilot.

The Remote Pilot Certificate itself does not have an expiration date — once issued, it remains valid indefinitely. However, to exercise the privileges of the certificate (i.e., to act as remote PIC for commercial operations), the pilot must complete recurrent training every 24 calendar months. This training can be completed online for free through the FAA Safety Team (FAASTeam) website, or by retaking the proctored knowledge test at a testing center. If recurrent training lapses, the certificate is not revoked or suspended — the pilot simply cannot legally act as remote PIC until the training is completed. There is no penalty for allowing recurrent training to lapse, and no requirement to retake the initial knowledge test. The pilot simply completes the recurrent training and immediately regains their privileges.

Remote pilots must keep their certificate available for inspection at all times during sUAS operations. This can be the physical certificate card or a digital copy accessed through IACRA. If asked by an FAA inspector, law enforcement officer, or other authorized official, the remote PIC must be able to present their certificate. Additionally, pilots must keep the FAA informed of changes to their personal information. A change of permanent address must be reported to the FAA within 30 days — failure to do so can result in enforcement action and may render the certificate invalid for exercising privileges. A change of legal name requires the pilot to apply for an updated certificate reflecting the new name. In both cases, updates are typically handled through the IACRA system or by contacting the FAA Airmen Certification Branch directly.

A remote pilot may voluntarily surrender their certificate at any time, for any reason. Voluntary surrender does not carry the same implications as an involuntary revocation — a pilot who voluntarily surrenders may later reapply for a new certificate by meeting all the standard requirements. Involuntary revocation, on the other hand, is an enforcement action taken by the FAA for serious violations (such as operating under the influence of drugs or alcohol, or committing fraud in connection with the certificate). A pilot whose certificate has been revoked must wait a specified period (typically one year) before reapplying, and must demonstrate that the conditions leading to revocation have been addressed.

Study Tip: A common exam question involves the consequences of failing to complete recurrent training on time. Remember: the certificate itself remains valid, but you cannot exercise its privileges until recurrent training is completed. This is different from a suspension or revocation, which are enforcement actions.
Exam Alert: You must notify the FAA within 30 days of a permanent change of address. Do not confuse this with the 10-day accident reporting timeframe. Address changes are reported through IACRA or by contacting the FAA Airmen Certification Branch.
Example: A remote pilot earned their certificate in June 2024 and completed recurrent training in May 2026. In September 2026, they move to a new state. They must notify the FAA of their new address within 30 days (by October 2026). Their next recurrent training is due by the end of May 2028 (24 calendar months from their last recurrent training). If they forget to complete recurrent training by May 2028, they cannot fly commercially until they complete it, but their certificate is not revoked — they simply need to finish the online course or retake the knowledge test.
