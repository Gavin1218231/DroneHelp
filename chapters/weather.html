Weather

Aviation weather theory, reading METARs and TAFs, density altitude, wind shear, and weather decision making.

Presentation: Aviation Weather for Drone Pilots

Why Weather Matters for Drone Pilots

Weather is one of the most critical factors in safe sUAS operations. Unlike manned aircraft, small drones are extremely sensitive to wind, temperature, and precipitation. Understanding weather allows you to:

  • Make informed go/no-go decisions before every flight
  • Anticipate how conditions will change during your operation
  • Maintain compliance with Part 107 visibility and cloud clearance requirements
  • Protect your equipment and avoid in-flight emergencies
Weather-related questions make up approximately 11–14% of the Part 107 knowledge test. You must be able to read METARs, TAFs, and understand density altitude.

The atmosphere is a dynamic system. Conditions can shift rapidly, especially at low altitudes where sUAS operate. A solid grasp of weather theory is essential for every remote pilot in command.

Atmosphere Basics

The atmosphere is the blanket of air surrounding the Earth. For aviation, we focus on the troposphere — the lowest layer, where nearly all weather occurs.

Standard Atmosphere (ISA)

  • Sea-level temperature: 15°C (59°F)
  • Sea-level pressure: 29.92 inHg (1013.25 hPa)
  • Temperature lapse rate: decreases approximately 2°C per 1,000 feet of altitude gain
  • Pressure lapse rate: approximately 1 inHg per 1,000 feet near sea level
The standard atmosphere is a theoretical model. Real conditions almost always differ. Deviations from standard are what create density altitude changes and affect aircraft performance.

Air density decreases with altitude, higher temperature, and higher humidity. Lower air density means reduced propeller efficiency and degraded drone performance.

Wind

Wind is the horizontal movement of air caused by pressure differences. It flows from areas of high pressure toward areas of low pressure.

Key Wind Concepts

  • Reported direction: Wind is always reported as the direction it blows FROM (e.g., 270° means wind comes from the west)
  • Speed units: Aviation uses knots (1 knot = 1.15 mph)
  • Gusts: Indicated by “G” in reports (e.g., 15G25KT = 15 knots, gusting to 25 knots)
  • Variable winds: Indicated by “VRB” when direction varies
Drone impact: Wind directly affects battery consumption, flight time, and controllability. Most small drones cannot safely operate in sustained winds above 20–25 knots. Always check gusts, not just sustained speed.

Crosswinds push a drone laterally and require constant correction. Headwinds reduce ground speed and drain battery faster. Tailwinds increase ground speed but can cause difficulty with precise hovering and landing.

Clouds and Visibility

Clouds form when moist air cools to its dew point. The type of cloud tells you about the atmospheric conditions that created it.

Primary Cloud Types

  • Cumulus: Puffy, cauliflower-shaped; indicate unstable air and convective activity
  • Stratus: Flat, layered; indicate stable air, often associated with steady precipitation and low ceilings
  • Cirrus: Thin, wispy, high-altitude (above 20,000 ft); made of ice crystals
  • Cumulonimbus (CB): Towering thunderstorm clouds; always avoid
Part 107 Cloud Clearance & Visibility Minimums:
• Visibility: 3 statute miles from the control station
• Cloud clearance: 500 feet below clouds, 1,000 feet above clouds, 2,000 feet horizontal from clouds

These minimums are not waivable for visibility but cloud clearance may be waived with proper authorization.

Temperature and Dew Point

The dew point is the temperature to which air must cool to become saturated. The relationship between temperature and dew point is one of the most important weather concepts for pilots.

Critical Relationships

  • When temperature and dew point are close together (spread of 4°F / 2°C or less), fog or low clouds are likely
  • When they converge, relative humidity approaches 100%
  • Temperature-dew point spread decreases as the surface cools (typically after sunset)
  • A small spread combined with light winds often produces radiation fog
Example: METAR reports Temp 08°C, Dew Point 07°C — spread is only 1°C. Fog or mist is very likely, especially near dawn. Flight visibility may drop below 3 SM minimums.

Relative humidity is the percentage of moisture in the air compared to the maximum it can hold at that temperature. Higher humidity also reduces air density, degrading drone performance.

Density Altitude

Density altitude is pressure altitude corrected for non-standard temperature. It represents the altitude at which the aircraft “thinks” it is flying based on air density.

The “3 H’s” of High Density Altitude

  • High altitude: Higher elevation airports/launch sites have thinner air
  • Hot temperatures: Warm air is less dense than cold air
  • Humid air: Water vapor is lighter than nitrogen and oxygen, so humid air is less dense
Performance effects: High density altitude reduces propeller efficiency, decreases maximum thrust, shortens flight time, and reduces payload capacity. A drone that performs well at sea level on a cool day may struggle at a high-elevation site on a hot afternoon.
Example: An airport at 5,000 ft MSL on a 95°F day may have a density altitude of 8,500 ft or higher. Your drone will perform as if it were at 8,500 ft in standard conditions.

Thunderstorms & Microbursts

Thunderstorms are among the most dangerous weather phenomena for all aircraft. Every thunderstorm contains severe turbulence, wind shear, and potentially hail and lightning.

Three Stages of a Thunderstorm

  • Cumulus (building) stage: Characterized by updrafts; the cloud grows vertically
  • Mature stage: Most dangerous; contains both updrafts and downdrafts, heaviest rain, hail, lightning, and possible tornadoes
  • Dissipating stage: Dominated by downdrafts; rainfall diminishes but hazards still exist
Microbursts: Intense, localized downdrafts that spread outward upon hitting the surface, producing wind speeds up to 45 knots. They can occur with or without rain and last 5–15 minutes. Microbursts are extremely dangerous at low altitudes.
Avoidance rule: Do not fly within 30 minutes of a thunderstorm passing through your operating area. Maintain at least 20 statute miles from any thunderstorm identified as severe.

Wind Shear & Temperature Inversions

Wind Shear

Wind shear is a sudden change in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance. It can occur at any altitude and is hazardous because it causes abrupt changes in airspeed and lift.

  • Can be horizontal (change in wind across the surface) or vertical (change in wind with altitude)
  • Common near thunderstorms, frontal boundaries, and temperature inversions
  • Low-level wind shear (below 2,000 ft AGL) is most dangerous for sUAS operations

Temperature Inversions

A temperature inversion occurs when a warm air layer sits above cooler air near the surface, reversing the normal lapse rate.

  • Trap pollutants, haze, and smoke near the surface, reducing visibility
  • Produce very stable air that resists vertical mixing
  • Cause significant wind shear at the top of the inversion layer
  • Most common on clear nights with calm winds (radiation inversions)
A drone climbing through the top of a temperature inversion may encounter sudden wind shear and turbulence as it transitions from calm surface air to faster winds aloft.

Reading METARs

A METAR (Meteorological Aerodrome Report) is an aviation routine weather observation. METARs are issued hourly (or more frequently as a SPECI when conditions change significantly).

METAR Format Breakdown

  • Report type: METAR or SPECI
  • Station ID: 4-letter ICAO identifier (e.g., KJFK)
  • Date/Time: Day of month + time in Zulu/UTC (e.g., 121856Z = 12th day, 18:56 UTC)
  • Wind: Direction (3 digits, true north) + speed in knots + gusts if present (e.g., 31012G20KT)
  • Visibility: In statute miles (e.g., 10SM = 10 statute miles)
  • Weather phenomena: Codes like RA (rain), SN (snow), FG (fog), BR (mist), TS (thunderstorm)
  • Cloud cover: SKC/CLR, FEW (1/8–2/8), SCT (3/8–4/8), BKN (5/8–7/8), OVC (8/8), followed by height in hundreds of feet AGL
  • Temperature/Dew point: In Celsius, separated by “/”; “M” prefix = minus (e.g., M04/M18 = −4°C / −18°C)
  • Altimeter setting: “A” followed by 4 digits in inches of mercury (e.g., A3038 = 30.38 inHg)

METAR Decoding Example

METAR KORD 121856Z 31012G20KT 10SM FEW250 M04/M18 A3038

Decoded:

  • METAR: Routine weather observation
  • KORD: Chicago O’Hare International Airport
  • 121856Z: 12th day of the month at 18:56 UTC (Zulu)
  • 31012G20KT: Wind from 310° (northwest) at 12 knots, gusting to 20 knots
  • 10SM: Visibility 10 statute miles
  • FEW250: Few clouds at 25,000 feet AGL
  • M04/M18: Temperature −4°C, Dew point −18°C (large spread = dry air)
  • A3038: Altimeter setting 30.38 inches of mercury
Flight assessment: Good VFR conditions. Visibility and cloud clearance exceed Part 107 minimums. The gusty northwest wind (up to 20 knots) is the primary concern — evaluate your drone’s wind tolerance before flying.

Reading TAFs

A TAF (Terminal Aerodrome Forecast) is a weather forecast for the area within 5 statute miles of an airport. TAFs are typically issued every 6 hours and cover a 24- or 30-hour period.

TAF Format

  • Header: TAF + station + issue date/time + valid period
  • Body: Same format as METAR (wind, visibility, weather, clouds)

Change Groups

  • FM (From): A permanent, significant change expected at the time specified (e.g., FM1800 = from 18:00Z)
  • TEMPO: Temporary fluctuations expected, lasting less than 1 hour at a time
  • BECMG: A gradual change expected during the time period specified
  • PROB: Probability of conditions occurring (e.g., PROB40 = 40% probability)
Example: TAF KJFK 121730Z 1218/1324 28010KT P6SM SKC FM130200 33015G25KT P6SM BKN040

Translation: Valid from 12th at 18Z to 13th at 24Z. Initial: wind 280 at 10 kt, visibility more than 6 SM, sky clear. From 13th at 02Z: wind 330 at 15 gusting 25 kt, visibility more than 6 SM, broken clouds at 4,000 ft.

Weather Resources for Remote Pilots

As a remote pilot in command, you are responsible for obtaining adequate weather information before every flight. Here are the primary sources:

Official Aviation Weather Sources

  • Aviation Weather Center (aviationweather.gov): METARs, TAFs, Prog Charts, AIRMETs, SIGMETs, and radar
  • 1800WxBrief (1800wxbrief.com): FAA-approved source for official weather briefings; includes standard, abbreviated, and outlook briefings
  • Convective SIGMETs: Warn of thunderstorms, tornadoes, and severe weather affecting a wide area
  • AIRMETs: Advisories for moderate icing (AIRMET Zulu), moderate turbulence (AIRMET Tango), and IFR/mountain obscuration (AIRMET Sierra)
Best practice: Check weather at least 1 hour before your planned operation, and recheck at the field before launch. Conditions can change rapidly. Have predefined personal minimums (e.g., maximum wind speed, minimum visibility) and stick to them.
The FAA expects Part 107 pilots to obtain weather information from official aviation sources — not just consumer weather apps. You should be comfortable reading METARs and TAFs.

Types of Fog

Fog is a ground-level cloud that significantly reduces visibility. Understanding the different types of fog helps you predict when and where it will form — and how long it will last.

Four Primary Fog Types

  • Radiation fog: Forms on clear, calm nights when the ground cools rapidly by radiating heat. The cold surface chills the air above it to the dew point. This is the most common type of fog. It typically “burns off” after sunrise as solar heating warms the surface.
  • Advection fog: Occurs when warm, moist air moves horizontally over a cooler surface. Unlike radiation fog, advection fog can form during the day and may persist for extended periods. It is especially common along coastlines where warm ocean air meets cold coastal waters.
  • Upslope fog: Forms when moist air is forced up sloping terrain by wind. As the air rises, it cools adiabatically and reaches saturation. Common in mountainous areas and can cover large regions.
  • Steam fog: Occurs when cold air passes over a body of warm water. Moisture evaporates from the water surface and immediately condenses in the cold air above, creating wispy columns of rising fog. Often seen over lakes and rivers in autumn and early winter.
Part 107 impact: Fog reduces visibility below the required 3 statute mile minimum. Monitor the temperature/dewpoint spread closely — when the spread narrows to within 2°C, expect fog formation. Always check current METAR observations and be prepared to cancel or delay your operation.

Frontal Weather & Pressure Systems

Fronts are boundaries between air masses of different temperatures and moisture content. The passage of a front brings the most significant and rapid weather changes you will encounter as a drone pilot.

Types of Fronts

  • Cold fronts: Fast-moving with a steep frontal slope. They produce a narrow but intense band of weather including thunderstorms, heavy rain, gusty winds, and turbulence. After passage, expect quick clearing with cooler, drier air and improved visibility.
  • Warm fronts: Slow-moving with a gradual slope. They produce a wide area of stratiform clouds, steady rain or drizzle, fog, and poor visibility that can persist for extended periods. Weather may deteriorate slowly over hundreds of miles ahead of the front.
  • Stationary fronts: Similar weather to warm fronts but can persist for days over the same area, bringing prolonged periods of clouds, precipitation, and reduced visibility.

Pressure Systems

  • Low pressure systems: Air flows counterclockwise (Northern Hemisphere) and inward toward the center. Associated with rising air, cloud formation, and precipitation. Generally poor flying conditions.
  • High pressure systems: Air flows clockwise (Northern Hemisphere) and outward from the center. Associated with sinking air, clear skies, and good visibility. Generally favorable flying conditions.
Remember: The passage of a front brings the most significant and rapid weather changes. Always check prognostic charts and forecasts for frontal movement before planning a drone operation. If a front is expected during your flight window, have a contingency plan ready.

Winds Aloft Forecasts (FB/FD)

The Winds and Temperatures Aloft Forecast (FB, formerly FD) provides predicted wind direction, wind speed, and temperature at specified altitudes above reporting stations. This is essential for understanding wind conditions your drone may encounter at altitude.

Decoding FB Reports

  • Station identifier: 3-letter station code (e.g., DEN, ORD, MIA)
  • Altitude levels: Data reported for 3,000, 6,000, 9,000, 12,000 ft and higher
  • Format: 4 or 6 digits — first 2 digits × 10 = wind direction (true north); next 2 digits = wind speed in knots; last 2 digits (if present) = temperature in Celsius
  • Example: 2714+03 = wind from 270° at 14 knots, temperature +3°C

Special Codes

  • 9900: Light and variable winds (less than 5 knots)
  • Direction > 36: Add 100 knots to the speed and subtract 50 from the direction. Example: 7545 = direction (75 − 50) × 10 = 250°, speed 45 + 100 = 145 knots
  • No data at 3,000 ft: Temperatures are not forecast within 2,500 ft of station elevation; wind data is not reported when the station elevation is within 1,500 ft of the altitude level
Important distinction: Winds aloft in FB reports are given in TRUE north direction, not magnetic. Surface winds reported in METARs also reference true north, but pilots should be aware that wind direction indicators at airports (ATIS, AWOS) may reference magnetic north. This difference matters when correlating reports.

Icing, Frost & Cold Weather Operations

Icing and frost are serious hazards for all aircraft, including small drones. Even a thin layer of frost or ice on propellers and airframe surfaces degrades performance and can lead to loss of control.

Types of Aircraft Icing

  • Clear ice: Forms from large supercooled water droplets; heavy, hard, and difficult to remove. Most dangerous type.
  • Rime ice: Forms from small supercooled droplets; rough, milky, opaque appearance. Lighter than clear ice but disrupts airflow.
  • Mixed ice: Combination of clear and rime; has characteristics of both types.

Conditions for Icing

  • Visible moisture (clouds, rain, drizzle) combined with temperatures at or below 0°C (32°F)
  • Most common between 0°C and −20°C
  • AIRMET Zulu warns of moderate icing conditions

Frost Effects

  • Disrupts the smooth airflow over propeller blades and airframe surfaces
  • Reduces lift, increases drag, and decreases thrust efficiency
  • Can form on a drone left outdoors overnight when surface temperature drops below the dew point and below freezing
Cold weather battery impact: Lithium polymer (LiPo) batteries lose significant capacity in cold temperatures. At 0°C, expect 20–30% reduced capacity. Below −10°C, batteries may fail to deliver adequate current. Keep batteries warm until just before flight and monitor voltage closely.
Critical rule: Any frost, ice, or snow on aircraft surfaces must be removed before flight. This applies to drones just as it does to manned aircraft. Even a thin frost layer on propeller blades significantly reduces efficiency and can cause dangerous vibration.

Weather Briefings & Aviation Weather Products

Obtaining a proper weather briefing is a fundamental responsibility for every remote pilot. The FAA provides several products and services to help pilots assess weather conditions.

Three Types of Weather Briefings

  • Standard briefing: Most comprehensive; used when you have not previously received any weather information. Covers adverse conditions, synopsis, current and forecast conditions, winds aloft, and NOTAMs.
  • Abbreviated briefing: Used to update a previous standard briefing or to obtain specific items only.
  • Outlook briefing: Requested when the planned departure is 6+ hours away; provides a general weather overview for preliminary planning.

PIREPs (Pilot Reports)

  • Real-time reports from pilots in flight describing actual conditions encountered
  • Include location, time, altitude, aircraft type, sky conditions, visibility, temperature, wind, turbulence, and icing
  • Particularly valuable because they describe conditions as actually experienced, not forecast

SIGMETs vs AIRMETs

  • SIGMET: Issued for severe weather hazards affecting ALL aircraft — severe icing, severe or extreme turbulence, dust storms, volcanic ash. Valid for 4 hours (6 hours for volcanic ash and hurricane SIGMETs).
  • Convective SIGMET: Issued for severe thunderstorms (surface winds ≥50 kt, hail ≥3/4 inch), tornadoes, or lines of thunderstorms. Valid for 2 hours.
  • AIRMET: Issued for moderate hazards — Sierra (IFR/mountain obscuration), Tango (turbulence, strong surface winds), Zulu (moderate icing). Valid for 6 hours.
Validity periods to memorize: SIGMETs are valid for 4 hours (6 hours for volcanic ash and hurricanes). Convective SIGMETs are valid for 2 hours. AIRMETs are valid for 6 hours. These validity periods are commonly tested on the Part 107 exam.

Atmospheric Stability & Air Masses

Atmospheric stability determines the type of weather you will encounter. Stability refers to the atmosphere’s resistance to vertical motion — whether a parcel of air, once displaced, tends to return to its original position (stable) or continue moving (unstable).

Stable Atmosphere Characteristics

  • Clouds: Stratiform (stratus, nimbostratus) — flat, layered
  • Precipitation: Steady, continuous rain or drizzle
  • Turbulence: Smooth air with little vertical mixing
  • Visibility: Poor — haze, fog, and smoke trapped near the surface

Unstable Atmosphere Characteristics

  • Clouds: Cumuliform (cumulus, cumulonimbus) — puffy, towering
  • Precipitation: Showery, intermittent, sometimes heavy
  • Turbulence: Moderate to severe; thermals and convective currents
  • Visibility: Generally good, except in precipitation

Determining Stability

  • Compare the environmental lapse rate to the standard lapse rate (2°C per 1,000 ft)
  • If temperature decreases faster than standard → unstable
  • If temperature decreases slower than standard (or increases = inversion) → stable

Air Mass Types

  • mT (maritime Tropical): Warm, moist — originates over tropical oceans; brings humidity and potential thunderstorms
  • mP (maritime Polar): Cool, moist — originates over cold oceans; brings clouds, rain, and reduced visibility
  • cT (continental Tropical): Hot, dry — originates over desert regions; brings high density altitude
  • cP (continental Polar): Cold, dry — originates over cold land masses; brings clear skies but extreme cold
Remember for the exam: An unstable atmosphere produces cumuliform clouds, turbulence, showery precipitation, and good visibility (except in storms). A stable atmosphere produces stratiform clouds, smooth air, continuous precipitation, and restricted visibility due to trapped haze and fog.

In-Depth Reading: Aviation Weather

Introduction to Aviation Weather

Weather is a leading factor in aviation accidents across all aircraft types, and small unmanned aircraft are no exception. Remote pilots must understand how weather affects their operations, how to interpret aviation weather products, and how to make sound go/no-go decisions. This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the weather topics covered on the Part 107 knowledge test and essential knowledge for safe sUAS operations.

Unlike manned aircraft pilots who may fly above or around weather, drone pilots operate at very low altitudes — typically below 400 feet AGL. This means you are directly affected by surface-level conditions: ground-hugging fog, thermals from heated surfaces, low-level wind shear, and turbulence caused by buildings and terrain. Understanding these phenomena is not just academic; it is a practical safety necessity.

The Atmosphere and Standard Conditions

The atmosphere is divided into layers. The troposphere, extending from the surface to approximately 36,000 feet in mid-latitudes, is where virtually all weather occurs. Temperature generally decreases with altitude in the troposphere at an average rate of about 2 degrees Celsius per 1,000 feet (known as the standard lapse rate).

The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) establishes baseline conditions for aviation calculations:

  • Temperature at sea level: 15 degrees Celsius (59 degrees Fahrenheit)
  • Pressure at sea level: 29.92 inches of mercury (inHg) or 1013.25 hectopascals (hPa)
  • Temperature lapse rate: approximately 2 degrees Celsius per 1,000 feet

When actual conditions differ from ISA, aircraft performance changes. Warmer-than-standard temperatures create higher density altitude and reduce performance. Colder-than-standard temperatures have the opposite effect.

Why this matters for the exam: Questions about the standard atmosphere, lapse rate, and pressure are common. Remember: 15 degrees Celsius, 29.92 inHg, and a lapse rate of 2 degrees Celsius per 1,000 feet.

Wind and Its Effects on Drone Operations

Wind is the horizontal movement of air from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure. In the Northern Hemisphere, the Coriolis effect causes winds to flow clockwise around high-pressure systems and counterclockwise around low-pressure systems.

Wind is always reported in aviation as the direction FROM which it blows, measured in degrees from true north, and speed is reported in knots. For example, a wind reported as 27015KT means wind from 270 degrees (west) at 15 knots.

For drone pilots, wind is one of the most limiting operational factors:

  • Headwinds reduce ground speed, requiring more power and draining battery faster. A drone flying into a 15-knot headwind uses significantly more energy than in calm conditions.
  • Crosswinds push the drone laterally and require constant correction inputs, which also increases power consumption.
  • Gusty winds are particularly hazardous because they create sudden, unpredictable forces. A drone may handle a steady 15-knot wind but could lose control in gusts to 25 knots.
  • Mechanical turbulence occurs when wind flows over and around obstacles such as buildings, trees, and terrain features. Expect turbulence on the downwind side of obstructions.
Practical tip: Fly your planned route INTO the wind at the start of your mission when battery is full. Return with a tailwind when battery is lower. This provides a safety margin. Always set personal wind limits that are below your drone manufacturer's maximum rated wind speed.

Cloud Types and Their Significance

Clouds are visible collections of water droplets or ice crystals suspended in the atmosphere. The type and altitude of clouds provide valuable information about current and forecast weather conditions.

Cloud Type Altitude Appearance Weather Significance
Cirrus (CI) Above 20,000 ft Thin, wispy, white Made of ice crystals; may indicate approaching front
Cirrostratus (CS) Above 20,000 ft Thin, sheet-like Creates halo around sun/moon; warm front approaching
Altocumulus (AC) 6,500–20,000 ft White/gray patches or rolls On a warm, humid morning, may indicate afternoon thunderstorms
Altostratus (AS) 6,500–20,000 ft Gray/blue sheet Sun dimly visible; continuous precipitation likely
Cumulus (CU) Below 6,500 ft Puffy, flat base Fair weather; indicate unstable air and convection
Stratus (ST) Below 6,500 ft Flat, gray, uniform Stable air; may produce drizzle; low ceilings
Stratocumulus (SC) Below 6,500 ft Lumpy, gray masses Usually does not produce significant precipitation
Nimbostratus (NS) Below 6,500 ft Dark, thick, shapeless Steady rain or snow; very low visibility
Cumulonimbus (CB) Low base to 60,000+ ft Towering, anvil top Thunderstorms; severe turbulence, hail, lightning, tornadoes
Cumulonimbus (CB) clouds are always hazardous. They produce thunderstorms with severe turbulence, lightning, hail, heavy rain, microbursts, and possible tornadoes. Never fly near cumulonimbus clouds.

Part 107 Weather Minimums

Under 14 CFR Part 107, the following weather minimums apply to all sUAS operations (unless a waiver has been obtained):

Requirement Minimum
Flight visibility (from control station) 3 statute miles
Distance below clouds 500 feet
Distance above clouds 1,000 feet
Horizontal distance from clouds 2,000 feet
Exam tip: These numbers appear frequently on the Part 107 test. The memory aid “5-1-2-3” can help: 500 below, 1,000 above, 2,000 horizontal, 3 SM visibility. Since Part 107 operations are limited to 400 feet AGL (or within 400 feet of a structure), the “1,000 feet above” requirement effectively means you cannot fly through clouds at all.

Types of Fog and Reduced Visibility

Fog is one of the most frequent causes of below-minimums visibility for drone operations. Because fog forms at ground level — exactly where sUAS operate — it poses a direct threat to legal and safe flight. Understanding how different fog types form allows you to anticipate visibility reductions before they occur. Radiation fog is the most common type and forms on clear, calm nights when the ground loses heat through longwave radiation, cooling the air immediately above the surface to its dew point. It is most prevalent in valleys and low-lying areas where cool air pools, and typically dissipates within a few hours of sunrise as solar heating warms the ground. Advection fog forms when warm, moist air flows over a cooler surface; unlike radiation fog, it can form at any time of day and may persist for long periods, making it especially hazardous along coastlines. Upslope fog develops when moist air is pushed up rising terrain and cools adiabatically to its dew point, and it is common in mountainous regions. Steam fog occurs when cold air moves over relatively warm water, causing rapid evaporation and immediate condensation into wispy, rising columns of fog.

The most reliable tool for predicting fog is the temperature-dewpoint spread. When the spread narrows to 2°C (approximately 4°F) or less and temperatures continue to fall, conditions are ripe for fog formation. Pilots should monitor the METAR trend closely: a steadily decreasing spread over successive observations is a strong signal that visibility will soon deteriorate. If fog forms or visibility drops rapidly between scheduled METAR reports, the weather station will issue a SPECI report (special weather observation). SPECI reports are triggered by significant changes in conditions — such as visibility dropping below key thresholds, the onset or cessation of precipitation, thunderstorm activity, or wind shifts — and they provide critical real-time updates that remote pilots should always check before and during operations.

Operational warning: Fog is one of the most common causes of below-minimums visibility for drone operations. Because it forms at the surface, fog can reduce visibility from several miles to a few hundred feet in a matter of minutes. If the temperature-dewpoint spread is 3°C or less and falling, seriously consider postponing your flight. Always have a plan to land immediately if visibility deteriorates unexpectedly during your operation.

Frontal Weather Systems

Frontal systems are boundaries between contrasting air masses, and they produce the most significant and rapid weather changes encountered in aviation. A cold front occurs when a cold air mass advances and forces warmer air aloft. Cold fronts move relatively quickly (15–30 knots typical), have a steep frontal slope, and produce a narrow but intense band of weather. Associated hazards include cumulonimbus clouds, thunderstorms with heavy rain and hail, severe turbulence, gusty and shifting surface winds, and low-level wind shear. However, once a cold front passes, conditions generally improve quickly with cooler temperatures, lower humidity, and excellent visibility. A warm front, by contrast, moves slowly as warm air gradually rides up and over retreating cooler air. This produces an extensive area of stratiform clouds (cirrus, then altostratus, then nimbostratus), steady precipitation, widespread fog, and poor visibility that can extend hundreds of miles ahead of the surface front and persist for 12–24 hours or more. Stationary fronts produce weather similar to warm fronts but can stall over an area for several days, creating prolonged periods of unsuitable flying conditions.

Several advisory products help remote pilots track hazardous weather associated with fronts. Convective SIGMETs are issued for the most dangerous convective weather: severe thunderstorms (surface winds 50 knots or greater, hail 3/4 inch or larger), tornadoes, and lines of thunderstorms. Each convective SIGMET is valid for 2 hours and covers a defined area. If a convective SIGMET is active in your area, do not fly. AIRMETs (Airmen’s Meteorological Information) are issued for conditions that are hazardous to all aircraft, including small UAS. There are three types: AIRMET Sierra warns of IFR conditions (visibility below 3 miles and/or ceilings below 1,000 feet) and mountain obscuration; AIRMET Tango warns of moderate turbulence and sustained surface winds of 30 knots or greater; and AIRMET Zulu warns of moderate icing conditions. For Part 107 pilots, AIRMET Sierra is particularly relevant because it directly addresses visibility and ceiling conditions that affect legal operations, while AIRMET Tango alerts you to wind conditions that can make small drone operations unsafe or impossible.

Temperature, Dew Point, and Fog

The dew point is the temperature at which air becomes fully saturated and water vapor begins to condense into visible moisture (clouds or fog). The difference between the current temperature and the dew point is called the temperature-dew point spread.

When the spread narrows to approximately 2 degrees Celsius (4 degrees Fahrenheit) or less, fog, mist, or low clouds become likely. If the temperature continues to fall and reaches the dew point, the air is saturated at 100% relative humidity.

Types of fog most relevant to drone operations:

  • Radiation fog: Forms on clear, calm nights when the ground cools and chills the air above it. Most common in valleys and low areas. Typically burns off after sunrise.
  • Advection fog: Forms when warm, moist air moves over a cooler surface. Can occur day or night and may persist for extended periods. Common along coastlines.
  • Upslope fog: Forms when moist air is pushed uphill by wind, cooling adiabatically. Common in mountainous terrain.
  • Precipitation-induced fog (frontal fog): Occurs when warm rain falls through a layer of cooler air, saturating it.
Planning tip: If the METAR shows a temperature-dew point spread of 3 degrees Celsius or less and temperatures are falling (such as in the late afternoon or evening), expect visibility to deteriorate. Plan to complete your mission before conditions drop below minimums.

Density Altitude: The Performance Killer

Density altitude is pressure altitude corrected for non-standard temperature. In simpler terms, it tells you the effective altitude your drone is operating at based on actual air density. Higher density altitude means thinner air and reduced performance.

Three factors increase density altitude (the “3 H’s”):

  1. High altitude: Higher elevation means lower atmospheric pressure and thinner air.
  2. Hot temperature: Warm air is less dense. On a hot day, density altitude may be thousands of feet higher than the actual elevation.
  3. Humid air: Contrary to intuition, humid air is LESS dense than dry air because water vapor molecules (molecular weight 18) replace heavier nitrogen (28) and oxygen (32) molecules.
Real-world example: A drone operator at a field elevation of 5,000 feet MSL on a summer day with a temperature of 35 degrees Celsius (95 degrees Fahrenheit) may face a density altitude of approximately 8,500 feet. The drone’s motors must work much harder to generate the same thrust, battery drain increases, maximum altitude capability decreases, and payload capacity is reduced.

To calculate density altitude, start with pressure altitude (elevation corrected for non-standard pressure) and adjust for temperature. The general rule: for every 1 degree Celsius above standard temperature at your altitude, add approximately 120 feet to pressure altitude to get density altitude.

Calculating pressure altitude: Pressure Altitude = Field Elevation + [(29.92 - Current Altimeter Setting) x 1,000]. For example, if your field elevation is 2,000 ft and the altimeter setting is 29.72 inHg: PA = 2,000 + [(29.92 - 29.72) x 1,000] = 2,000 + 200 = 2,200 ft.

Thunderstorms: Identification and Avoidance

Thunderstorms are the most dangerous weather phenomenon for any aircraft. Three ingredients are required for thunderstorm formation: sufficient moisture, an unstable atmosphere, and a lifting mechanism (fronts, terrain, daytime heating).

Every thunderstorm progresses through three stages:

  1. Cumulus (developing) stage: Characterized by strong updrafts. The cloud builds rapidly upward. No precipitation reaches the ground yet, but conditions are deteriorating.
  2. Mature stage: The most hazardous phase. Both updrafts and downdrafts exist within the storm. Heavy rain, hail, lightning, severe turbulence, and microbursts occur. Tornadoes are most likely during this stage.
  3. Dissipating stage: Downdrafts dominate. Precipitation weakens. However, hazards such as turbulence, low-level wind shear, and lightning continue.

Thunderstorm avoidance rules for drone pilots:

  • Never fly within or near a thunderstorm at any stage.
  • Wait at least 30 minutes after the last observed lightning, thunder, or heavy rain before resuming operations.
  • Stay at least 20 statute miles away from any thunderstorm classified as severe (winds 50+ knots, hail 1 inch or larger, or tornadoes).
  • Be aware that anvil clouds can extend 20+ miles from the main cell and may produce lightning and hail.
  • Gust fronts can extend 15 or more miles ahead of a thunderstorm, bringing sudden wind shifts and dangerous turbulence.
Microbursts are concentrated downdrafts within or near thunderstorms that produce damaging outflow winds at the surface. A microburst can produce wind speeds of 45 knots or more over an area as small as 1–2 miles across. Microbursts may occur with or without visible precipitation (wet or dry microbursts). They typically last 5 to 15 minutes but can destroy a drone instantly.

Temperature Inversions and Low-Level Effects

A temperature inversion occurs when air temperature increases with altitude instead of decreasing as is normal. This creates a very stable layer that acts like a lid on the atmosphere.

Effects of temperature inversions on drone operations:

  • Trapped pollution and haze: The inversion traps particles, smoke, and haze below it, significantly reducing surface visibility. This may prevent operations that require 3 SM visibility.
  • Low-level wind shear: Wind speed and direction can change dramatically at the top of the inversion layer. A drone flying through this transition may experience sudden, unexpected turbulence.
  • Fog formation: Surface-based inversions (especially radiation inversions on clear, calm nights) are prime conditions for fog development.
  • Smooth surface air with turbulence aloft: Conditions may appear calm at the surface, but significant wind and turbulence may exist just a few hundred feet up at the inversion boundary.
Recognition: Temperature inversions are indicated on METARs when the temperature-dew point spread is small, visibility is reduced due to haze or fog, and winds are light at the surface. On sounding charts (Skew-T diagrams), inversions appear where the temperature line slopes to the right (increasing) with altitude.

TAF Decoding Guide

A TAF (Terminal Aerodrome Forecast) provides expected weather conditions over a 24- or 30-hour period. It uses the same codes as METARs but includes forecast time periods and change indicators.

TAF structure:

  1. Header: TAF [AMD] KXXX DDHHMM Z DDHH/DDHH — identifies the station, issue time, and valid period
  2. Initial forecast: Expected conditions at the beginning of the valid period
  3. Change groups: Describe how conditions are expected to change

Change group details:

  • FM (From): Indicates a rapid, significant, and sustained change. All conditions after FM replace previous conditions. Example: FM182000 25012KT P6SM SCT040 — From the 18th at 20:00Z, wind 250 at 12 knots, visibility greater than 6 SM, scattered clouds at 4,000 ft.
  • TEMPO: Temporary fluctuations expected to last less than one hour at a time and occur during less than half the time period. Example: TEMPO 1820/1824 3SM TSRA BKN020CB — Temporarily between 18th 20Z and 18th 24Z, visibility 3 SM in thunderstorm and rain, broken cumulonimbus at 2,000 ft.
  • BECMG: A gradual or permanent change expected during the specified time period. Example: BECMG 1814/1816 31015KT — Becoming between 18th 14Z and 18th 16Z, wind 310 at 15 knots.
  • PROB: Probability of occurrence (typically PROB30 or PROB40). PROB30 means a 30% chance. Example: PROB40 1802/1806 1SM +TSRA — 40% probability between 18th 02Z and 18th 06Z of visibility 1 SM in heavy thunderstorm and rain.
Exam tip: When interpreting a TAF, read it sequentially. Start with the initial forecast and apply each change group in order. FM groups completely replace all previous conditions. TEMPO conditions are temporary deviations from the prevailing forecast. BECMG transitions happen gradually during the specified window.

How to Get a Weather Briefing

The FAA expects all pilots, including remote pilots, to obtain adequate weather information before flight. For Part 107 operations, the following resources are available:

  • 1800WxBrief.com (or call 1-800-WX-BRIEF): The FAA-contracted flight service provider. Offers three types of briefings:
    • Standard briefing: Most complete; requested when you have not received a previous briefing. Includes adverse conditions, current and forecast weather, winds aloft, NOTAMs, and ATC delays.
    • Abbreviated briefing: Requested when you need to update a previous briefing or only need specific information.
    • Outlook briefing: Requested when your planned flight is 6+ hours away. Provides a general overview of expected conditions.
  • Aviation Weather Center (aviationweather.gov): Provides METARs, TAFs, prognostic charts, radar, satellite imagery, AIRMETs, SIGMETs, and convective SIGMETs.
  • Notices to Air Missions (NOTAMs): While not strictly weather, NOTAMs may include temporary flight restrictions (TFRs) related to weather events such as firefighting or disaster response.
Important: Consumer weather apps and websites (phone weather apps, Weather.com, etc.) can supplement your planning but should NOT be your primary source for aviation weather. They lack critical information like cloud heights, visibility in statute miles, winds at altitude, and aviation-specific hazards. Always use official aviation weather sources.

Weather Decision Making

Good weather decision making is about setting personal minimums, recognizing deteriorating conditions, and being willing to cancel or postpone a flight. Consider establishing the following personal minimums before every mission:

  • Wind limits: Set a maximum sustained wind speed and gust factor for your specific drone. Consider that manufacturer specs are tested in ideal conditions.
  • Visibility limits: The legal minimum is 3 SM, but consider requiring higher visibility for certain missions, especially those near other aircraft or obstacles.
  • Precipitation: Most consumer and commercial drones are not water-resistant. Any precipitation should ground your operation.
  • Temperature limits: Lithium batteries perform poorly in very cold temperatures (below 32 degrees Fahrenheit / 0 degrees Celsius) and may have reduced capacity by 20% or more. Extreme heat degrades battery life and can cause overheating.
  • Density altitude awareness: If operating at high elevations or in hot conditions, reduce payload and planned flight time to account for decreased performance.
The PAVE checklist is a useful personal minimums framework: Pilot (Am I fit to fly?), Aircraft (Is the drone airworthy?), enVironment (Weather, airspace, terrain), External pressures (Am I being pressured to fly in marginal conditions?). If any element is unsatisfactory, do not fly.

Winds Aloft Forecasts (FB/FD)

The Winds and Temperatures Aloft Forecast, designated FB (formerly FD), is an essential aviation weather product that provides predicted wind direction, wind speed, and temperature at specific altitudes above selected reporting stations. These forecasts are issued four times daily and are valid for 6- and 12-hour periods. For Part 107 remote pilots, FB reports are valuable because surface winds alone do not tell the whole story — wind conditions can change dramatically within the first few hundred feet of altitude. A drone launching in calm surface winds may encounter significantly stronger winds at 200 or 300 feet AGL, and the FB report helps you anticipate this.

Decoding an FB report requires understanding the data format. Each station lists wind and temperature data for standard altitude levels: 3,000, 6,000, 9,000, 12,000 feet, and higher. The data at each level is presented as a group of 4 or 6 digits. The first two digits, multiplied by 10, give the wind direction in degrees TRUE (not magnetic). The next two digits give the wind speed in knots. If a 6-digit group is present, the last two digits represent the temperature in degrees Celsius (a minus (negative) sign is implied above 24,000 ft, where temperatures are always negative; below that level, the sign is included).

FB Code Wind Direction Wind Speed Temperature
2714+03 27 × 10 = 270° (west) 14 knots +3°C
3425-08 34 × 10 = 340° (north-northwest) 25 knots −8°C
9900 Light and variable (below 5 knots) N/A
7545 (75 − 50) × 10 = 250° 45 + 100 = 145 knots N/A

Two special codes appear frequently and are tested on the Part 107 exam. The code 9900 means “light and variable” — winds are less than 5 knots with no consistent direction. When the first two digits of the direction exceed 36, it signals a high-speed encoding: subtract 50 from the direction digits and add 100 to the speed digits. For example, 7545 decodes as direction (75 − 50) × 10 = 250°, speed 45 + 100 = 145 knots. Additionally, temperature data is not forecast for altitudes within 2,500 feet of the station elevation, and wind data is omitted entirely when the station elevation is within 1,500 feet of the altitude level.

Exam tip: A common Part 107 test question asks you to decode an FB entry. Practice converting the coded groups: remember that direction digits are multiplied by 10, the code 9900 means light and variable, and direction values above 36 use the special high-speed encoding. Also remember that FB winds are referenced to TRUE north, which differs from how wind direction indicators at airports may reference magnetic north.

Icing, Frost & Cold Weather Operations

Icing is a significant hazard for all aircraft, and small drones are not immune. Structural icing occurs when supercooled water droplets (liquid water existing below 0°C) strike an aircraft surface and freeze on contact. There are three types of structural icing. Clear ice forms from large supercooled droplets or freezing rain; it is smooth, heavy, hard to see, and extremely difficult to remove — making it the most dangerous type. Rime ice forms from small supercooled droplets that freeze rapidly on contact, creating a rough, milky, opaque accumulation. While lighter than clear ice, rime ice disrupts smooth airflow over surfaces. Mixed ice is a combination of clear and rime, with properties of both. Even a thin coating of any ice type on drone propeller blades can significantly alter their aerodynamic profile, causing vibration, reduced thrust, and potential loss of control.

The conditions necessary for icing are straightforward: visible moisture (clouds, precipitation, drizzle) combined with temperatures at or below 0°C (32°F). The most hazardous icing zone is generally between 0°C and −20°C, because below −20°C most moisture has already frozen into ice crystals, which are less likely to adhere to surfaces. AIRMET Zulu is the advisory product that warns of moderate icing conditions, and any active AIRMET Zulu in your area should prompt serious reconsideration of your flight plan.

Frost is a related but distinct hazard. It forms when the surface temperature of an object drops below the dew point and below freezing — moisture deposits directly as ice crystals on the surface. Even a thin layer of frost on propeller blades and airframe surfaces disrupts the smooth airflow that is essential for generating lift and thrust. Frost increases drag and reduces propeller efficiency, which can lead to degraded climb performance, increased power consumption, and potential control difficulties. The critical rule, tested repeatedly on the Part 107 exam, is simple: any frost, ice, or snow must be removed from all aircraft surfaces before flight. This applies to drones just as it does to manned aircraft.

Cold weather battery warning: Lithium polymer (LiPo) batteries are severely affected by cold temperatures. At 0°C (32°F), expect a 20–30% reduction in available capacity. Below −10°C (14°F), batteries may fail to deliver adequate current for stable flight. Best practices include: storing batteries in an insulated container until just before flight, pre-warming batteries to at least 20°C before use, shortening planned flight times to account for reduced capacity, and monitoring battery voltage closely throughout the flight. A sudden voltage drop in cold conditions can cause an unplanned emergency landing or crash.
Exam scenario: A Part 107 test question may describe a drone left outdoors overnight during winter and ask what must be done before flight. The answer is always that all frost, ice, and snow must be completely removed from all surfaces. Another common question involves identifying AIRMET Zulu as the advisory for moderate icing conditions (as opposed to AIRMET Sierra for IFR/mountain obscuration and AIRMET Tango for turbulence).

Weather Briefings & Aviation Weather Products

Obtaining a thorough weather briefing is one of the most fundamental responsibilities of a remote pilot in command. The FAA provides weather briefing services through 1800WxBrief (operated by Leidos Flight Service), accessible online at 1800wxbrief.com or by telephone at 1-800-WX-BRIEF. Three types of briefings are available. A Standard briefing is the most comprehensive and should be requested when you have not previously received any weather information for your planned operation; it covers adverse conditions, weather synopsis, current conditions, forecast conditions, winds aloft, NOTAMs, and ATC delays. An Abbreviated briefing is used to supplement or update a previous standard briefing, or when you need only specific items of weather data. An Outlook briefing is appropriate when your planned flight is 6 or more hours away; it provides general weather trends and expected conditions for preliminary planning purposes, with the understanding that you will request a standard or abbreviated briefing closer to your flight time.

PIREPs (Pilot Reports) are among the most valuable weather products because they describe conditions as actually experienced by pilots in flight, rather than forecast or remotely observed. A PIREP includes the reporting pilot’s location, time, altitude, aircraft type, sky conditions, flight visibility, temperature, wind, turbulence intensity, and icing type and intensity. For remote pilots, PIREPs provide real-world confirmation of what conditions are actually like at low altitudes in your area. They are especially useful for verifying or contradicting forecast products.

Several graphical weather products are important for Part 107 exam preparation. Surface Analysis Charts show current positions of fronts, pressure systems, and surface observations, providing a snapshot of current conditions. Prognostic (Prog) Charts are forecast charts showing expected positions of fronts, pressure systems, and areas of precipitation at 12, 24, 36, and 48 hours into the future. Significant Weather Charts depict areas of IFR and MVFR conditions, turbulence, and freezing levels. These products help remote pilots develop a “big picture” understanding of the weather patterns affecting their area.

Product Type Validity Period Covers

Convective SIGMET Warning — all aircraft 2 hours Severe thunderstorms (surface winds ≥50 kt, hail ≥3/4 inch), tornadoes, lines of thunderstorms

Memorize these validity periods for the exam: SIGMETs are valid for 4 hours (except volcanic ash and hurricane SIGMETs, which are valid for 6 hours). Convective SIGMETs are valid for 2 hours. AIRMETs are valid for 6 hours. A key distinction: SIGMETs address severe hazards dangerous to ALL aircraft, while AIRMETs address moderate hazards. Convective SIGMETs specifically address severe thunderstorm activity.
Exam tip: The Part 107 test frequently asks about the difference between SIGMETs and AIRMETs. Remember: SIGMET = Significant Meteorological Information = severe hazards for ALL aircraft. AIRMET = Airmen’s Meteorological Information = moderate hazards. If a question describes severe icing or severe turbulence, the answer is SIGMET. If it describes moderate icing or moderate turbulence, the answer is AIRMET.

Atmospheric Stability & Air Masses

Atmospheric stability is a foundational concept that determines the character of the weather you will encounter. Stability refers to the atmosphere’s tendency to resist or enhance vertical motion. In a stable atmosphere, a parcel of air that is displaced vertically tends to return to its original position because it is cooler (and therefore denser) than the surrounding air. This suppresses vertical motion and produces characteristic weather: stratiform clouds (stratus, nimbostratus, altostratus) with flat, layered appearances; smooth air with little turbulence; continuous, steady precipitation (rain or drizzle); and poor visibility caused by haze, fog, and smoke trapped near the surface. The very smoothness of stable air is deceptive — while it may feel comfortable to fly in, the restricted visibility can easily drop below Part 107 minimums.

In an unstable atmosphere, a displaced air parcel continues to rise because it remains warmer (less dense) than the surrounding environment. This enhances vertical motion and produces dramatically different weather: cumuliform clouds (cumulus, towering cumulus, cumulonimbus) that develop vertically; moderate to severe turbulence from convective currents and thermals; showery, intermittent precipitation that can be heavy; and generally good visibility between showers because vertical mixing disperses haze and pollutants. However, visibility within precipitation can be extremely poor, and the turbulence associated with instability poses a direct threat to small drone operations.

Stability is determined by comparing the environmental lapse rate (the actual rate at which temperature decreases with altitude) to the standard lapse rate of approximately 2°C per 1,000 feet. If the environmental temperature decreases faster than the standard rate, the atmosphere is unstable. If it decreases more slowly (or increases, as in a temperature inversion), the atmosphere is stable. Temperature inversions represent the most extremely stable conditions possible.

Characteristic Stable Atmosphere Unstable Atmosphere
Cloud type Stratiform (flat, layered) Cumuliform (puffy, towering)
Turbulence Smooth air Moderate to severe; thermals
Precipitation Continuous, steady Showery, intermittent
Visibility Poor (haze, fog, smoke trapped) Good (except in precipitation)
Lapse rate Less than standard (or inversion) Greater than standard

Air masses are large bodies of air with relatively uniform temperature and moisture characteristics, determined by the region where the air mass formed. Four primary air mass types affect the United States: mT (maritime Tropical) — warm and moist, originating over tropical oceans, bringing humidity, haze, and potential thunderstorms; mP (maritime Polar) — cool and moist, originating over cold ocean waters, bringing clouds, rain, and reduced visibility; cT (continental Tropical) — hot and dry, originating over desert regions like the American Southwest, creating high density altitude and thermal turbulence; and cP (continental Polar) — cold and dry, originating over northern land masses like Canada, bringing very cold temperatures with clear skies but significant battery performance concerns for drone operators.

Remember for the exam: The characteristics of stable vs. unstable air are among the most frequently tested weather topics on the Part 107 knowledge test. An unstable atmosphere produces cumuliform clouds, turbulence, showery precipitation, and good visibility (except in storms). A stable atmosphere produces stratiform clouds, smooth air, continuous precipitation, and restricted visibility due to trapped haze and fog. If a question describes layered clouds with steady drizzle and haze, the atmosphere is stable. If it describes towering clouds with rain showers and bumpy air, the atmosphere is unstable.
Practical application: When planning a drone mission, consider atmospheric stability. On a stable day with haze, your primary concern is visibility — can you maintain the 3 SM minimum and keep visual line of sight with your drone? On an unstable day with cumulus development, your primary concerns are turbulence and sudden wind gusts that could affect control, plus the potential for rapid thunderstorm development in the afternoon. Both conditions require different risk mitigation strategies.
